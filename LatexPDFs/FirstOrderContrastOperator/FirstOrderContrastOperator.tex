\title{\bfseries Efficient Modelling of Lateral Heterogeneity on Depth
Stratification via the Impedance Marching\\[4pt]
\large From the elastodynamic matrix--vector wave equation to a laterally
coupled\\ Dirichlet-to-Neumann march: derivation, validation, and what it
actually buys}
\author{}
\date{17 September 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
The elastodynamic matrix--vector wave equation $\partial_3\bq = \Aop\bq + \bd$
expresses the wave field as a first-order recursion in depth.  This note asks
whether the cube moment method can be applied to it directly, to obtain a
single-site $T$-matrix without passing through the Navier equation.  The answer
is yes, and the construction is not new: the thesis already carries it in
$2\tfrac12$-D.  What is new here is the $3$-D Cartesian operator, and a
measurement of what the route is worth.

Three results.  \emph{First}, the operator matrix $\Aop$ is derived from the
stiffness tensor and reproduces the thesis system matrix (Akdef) under one
constant similarity transform; the paper's $\nu_1,\nu_2$ are the thesis's
$\zeta,\chi$.  A fourth, arrived at late and by the wrong road, is that the
thesis's own spectral representation of $\bA$ --- analytic eigenvectors, a
piecewise branch, and an inverse obtained from the symplectic structure rather
than by inversion --- removes three numerical hazards outright that the operator
route had to discover and repair one at a time.  \emph{Second}, the test space is
\emph{derived} rather than chosen: $\Jsix$ is antisymmetric and
$\dA\tp\Jsix = -\Jsix\dA$, so $\Jsix\dA$ is symmetric and the only natural
projection is $\text{test} = \Jsix\times\text{trial}$.  This dissolves an
apparent mismatch of ten conditions against nine unknowns.  \emph{Third}, and
least expected, the resulting coupling agrees with the ordinary Navier weak form
at Born order but differs at $O(\Delta c^{2})$ entirely within the traction
channel --- and that difference splits into a genuine $\Oh$-invariant
resummation \emph{and} a spurious preference for the $x_3$ axis, in comparable
amounts.  A single-site score against an independent arbiter in a homogeneous
background would mix the two and settle neither.  The directional preference
ceases to be an artefact once the background is layered, which is where the
route was wanted.

The $T$-matrix itself is assembled in \S\ref{sec:tmatrix} and the comparison is
reported channel by channel.  The sharpest statement is not a norm: $e_{12}$ and
$e_{13}$ are equivalent under $\Oh$ and so must amplify identically, and in the
first-order $T$ they split by $8.4\times10^{-4}$ while the ordinary route splits
them by exactly zero.  That split is an arbiter-free \emph{lower bound} on the
first-order error, $\ge\delta/2$; it bounds nothing about the ordinary route,
which can be symmetrically wrong, so no ordering of the two follows from it.

The ordering is settled elsewhere, in the one geometry where both formulations
apply and an external reference exists.  A periodic patch of space-filling cubes
\emph{is} a homogeneous layer, so a reflectivity recursion gives its reflection
coefficient exactly; running both routes through the same lattice sum and the
same solve, with only $T_0$ substituted, the first-order $T$ is the more accurate
of the two, by a margin that grows with contrast and that \emph{grows further as
the shared lattice error is refined away} --- which is what distinguishes a real
advantage from an accidental cancellation (\S\ref{sec:whichbetter}).

That first-order system is then used for what the title names.  Because the
kernel is one-sided in depth, the integral equation splits and the problem
becomes an initial-value march in the impedance $\bY$, defined by
$\bta_3=\bY\bu$ --- the \emph{Dirichlet-to-Neumann map} of everything below the
current depth, which \S\ref{sec:dtn} argues is the single idea the rest of this
note keeps meeting under other names.  Marching $\bY$ rather than a reflection
matrix removes the mode decomposition entirely, and with it the branch loss that
limits the mode-basis form; carrying the lateral grid \emph{inside} the marched
variable then handles lateral heterogeneity without an outer iteration.  Against
an exact Mie sphere the march is fourth order in depth, and its residual is not
error but the multiple scattering of the periodic array, linear in contrast at
$1.45\,\varepsilon$ over two decades.  \S\ref{sec:priorart} sets out the
substantial prior art --- the construction is that of Haines, Hulme \& Yu
(2004) --- and \S\ref{sec:tricks} adopts and measures three of their
computational devices.

Every claim below is backed by an executable check; the scripts report $25/25$,
$44/44$, $14/14$, $17/17$, $9/9$, $7/7$, $7/7$ and $11/11$, and a separate gate binds the
numerical propagator to the derived $\Aop$.  Section~\ref{sec:open} states
plainly what is \emph{not} yet established.
\end{abstract}

\section{Conventions and objects}

\subsection{Indices, time, and units}

The positive $x_3$ axis points downward; $x_1,x_2$ are lateral.  Greek indices
$\alpha,\beta$ run over the lateral pair $\{1,2\}$ only, Latin indices over
$\{1,2,3\}$.  The time convention is $e^{-i\omega t}$ throughout, so
$\partial_t \to -i\omega$ and the particle velocity is $\bv = -i\omega\bu$.  The
lateral transform is $e^{+i(k_1x_1+k_2x_2)}$, hence $\partial_\alpha \to
ik_\alpha$.

Numerical work uses seismic units: km, km\,s$^{-1}$, g\,cm$^{-3}$, GPa.  These
are consistent, since $\text{GPa} = \text{g\,cm}^{-3}\cdot(\text{km\,s}^{-1})^2$.

Two orderings appear in the sources and must not be conflated.  The paper orders
components $(1,2,3)$; the thesis orders them $(z,x,y) = (x_3,x_1,x_2)$.  The map
between them is written explicitly in \S\ref{sec:arbiterB}.

\subsection{The field vector}

The matrix--vector wave equation is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvwe}
  \partial_3\bq = \Aop\,\bq + \bd,
  \qquad
  \bq = \begin{pmatrix}\bq_1\\ \bq_2\end{pmatrix},
  \quad
  \Aop = \begin{pmatrix}\Aop_{11} & \Aop_{12}\\ \Aop_{21} & \Aop_{22}\end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
with the elastodynamic choice
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qdef}
  \bq_1 = -\bta_3, \qquad \bq_2 = \bv,
  \qquad (\bta_j)_i = \tau_{ij},
\end{equation}
so that $\bq = (-\tau_{13},\,-\tau_{23},\,-\tau_{33},\,v_1,\,v_2,\,v_3)\tp$.
This is not an arbitrary pairing: $-\bta_3$ and $\bv$ are conjugate in the
vertical power flux, $j = \tfrac14(-\bta_3^{\dagger}\bv - \bv^{\dagger}\bta_3)$,
and that is what makes the metric of \S\ref{sec:metric} the natural one.

\paragraph{Transpose, and why it is marked this way.}  $(\cdot)\tp$ is the
transpose throughout and $(\cdot)^{\dagger}$ the conjugate transpose.  Neither
is ever a power.  The upright sans-serif marker is used deliberately: this note
carries $\bta_3$ as half of its own state vector, so a transpose written
$(\cdot)^{\top}$ sits a symbol next to $\tau$ that is hard to tell from it, and
one written $(\cdot)^{t}$ collides with the time of the $e^{-i\omega t}$
convention.  Earlier drafts used both, and inconsistently.

\subsection{The metric \texorpdfstring{$\Jsix$}{J6}}\label{sec:metric}

The symplectic metric is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:J6}
  \Jsix = \begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0} & \bI_3\\ -\bI_3 & \mathbf{0}\end{pmatrix},
  \qquad \Jsix\tp = -\Jsix,
  \qquad \Jsix^{2} = -\bI_6 .
\end{equation}

\paragraph{A naming warning.}  This project, following the thesis, calls this
matrix $\Jsix$; it is already defined as such in the package.  The paper calls
the \emph{same} matrix $\mathbf{N}$, and uses $\mathbf{J}$ for
$\operatorname{diag}(\bI_3,-\bI_3)$, a different object.  Translate on the way
in.  Every occurrence of $\mathbf{N}$ in the paper's equations (16)--(30)
corresponds to $\Jsix$ here.

The operator matrix is \emph{quasi-Hamiltonian} with respect to it,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:quasiham}
  \Aop\tp\Jsix = -\Jsix\Aop,
\end{equation}
which is the paper's eq.~(27) and the thesis's \texttt{ATdef}.  Everything in
\S\ref{sec:testspace} follows from \eqref{eq:quasiham} together with the
antisymmetry of $\Jsix$, and from nothing else.

\section{The first-order system for an isotropic medium}

\subsection{From the stiffness tensor to the stiffness matrices}

For an isotropic solid,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cijkl}
  c_{ijkl} = \lambda\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}
           + \mu(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl} + \delta_{il}\delta_{jk}),
  \qquad \rho_{ij} = \rho\,\delta_{ij},
\end{equation}
with $\lambda = \lambda(\mathbf{x},\omega)$, $\mu = \mu(\mathbf{x},\omega)$,
$\rho = \rho(\mathbf{x},\omega)$ and $K_c = \lambda + 2\mu$.  The stiffness
matrices are $(\bC_{jl})_{ik} = c_{ijkl}$.  In particular
\begin{equation}
  \bC_{33} = \operatorname{diag}(\mu,\mu,K_c),
  \qquad
  \bC_{33}^{-1} = \operatorname{diag}(\mu^{-1},\mu^{-1},K_c^{-1}),
\end{equation}
and $\bC_{jl}\tp = \bC_{lj}$, which follows from $c_{ijkl} = c_{klij}$.  All
nine blocks are reproduced from \eqref{eq:cijkl} and checked entry by entry.

\subsection{Eliminating the in-plane tractions}

The components $\bta_1,\bta_2$ do not appear in \eqref{eq:qdef} and must be
removed.  Doing so introduces the reduced stiffness
\begin{equation}\label{eq:U}
  \bU_{\alpha l} = \bC_{\alpha l} - \bC_{\alpha 3}\bC_{33}^{-1}\bC_{3l},
\end{equation}
with the two structural properties $\bU_{\alpha 3} = \mathbf{0}$ and
$\bU_{\alpha\beta}\tp = \bU_{\beta\alpha}$.  Both are checked rather than
assumed, because it is they --- not the algebra that produced them --- that make
the symmetry relations of \S\ref{sec:symrel} hold.

The isotropic $\bU_{\alpha\beta}$ is where two constants are \emph{born}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nu}
  \nu_1 \equiv (\bU_{11})_{11} = \frac{4\mu(\lambda+\mu)}{\lambda+2\mu},
  \qquad
  \nu_2 \equiv (\bU_{12})_{12} = \frac{2\mu\lambda}{\lambda+2\mu},
  \qquad
  \nu_1 - \nu_2 = 2\mu .
\end{equation}
They are read off $\bU$ and only then compared with the published definitions;
they are not independent quantities.  Every $\bU_{\alpha\beta}$ has a null third
row and column, which is the statement that $\tau_{33}$ has been fully
eliminated.

\subsection{The operator matrices}

The general construction gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Ablocks}
  \Aop_{11} = -\partial_\alpha\,\bC_{\alpha3}\bC_{33}^{-1},
  \quad
  \Aop_{12} = i\omega\rho - \tfrac{1}{i\omega}\,\partial_\alpha\bU_{\alpha\beta}\partial_\beta,
  \quad
  \Aop_{21} = i\omega\,\bC_{33}^{-1},
  \quad
  \Aop_{22} = -\bC_{33}^{-1}\bC_{3\beta}\,\partial_\beta,
\end{equation}
and for the isotropic medium these evaluate to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:A11A22}
  \Aop_{11} =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    0 & 0 & -\partial_1\tfrac{\lambda}{\lambda+2\mu}\\
    0 & 0 & -\partial_2\tfrac{\lambda}{\lambda+2\mu}\\
    -\partial_1 & -\partial_2 & 0
  \end{pmatrix},
  \qquad
  \Aop_{22} =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    0 & 0 & -\partial_1\\
    0 & 0 & -\partial_2\\
    -\tfrac{\lambda}{\lambda+2\mu}\partial_1 & -\tfrac{\lambda}{\lambda+2\mu}\partial_2 & 0
  \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:A12A21}
  \Aop_{12} =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    i\omega\rho - \tfrac{1}{i\omega}(\partial_1\nu_1\partial_1 + \partial_2\mu\partial_2)
      & -\tfrac{1}{i\omega}(\partial_2\mu\partial_1 + \partial_1\nu_2\partial_2) & 0\\
    -\tfrac{1}{i\omega}(\partial_2\nu_2\partial_1 + \partial_1\mu\partial_2)
      & i\omega\rho - \tfrac{1}{i\omega}(\partial_1\mu\partial_1 + \partial_2\nu_1\partial_2) & 0\\
    0 & 0 & i\omega\rho
  \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
with $\Aop_{21} = i\omega\operatorname{diag}(\mu^{-1},\mu^{-1},K_c^{-1})$.

\paragraph{The ordering is the content.}  Compare the $(1,3)$ entry of
$\Aop_{11}$ with the $(3,1)$ entry of $\Aop_{22}$.  In the first the derivative
stands to the \emph{left} of the modulus and differentiates the product; in the
second it stands to the \emph{right} and differentiates the field alone.  For a
laterally invariant medium the distinction collapses and is invisible.  For a
laterally varying one it is the physics.  This is why the checks in
\S\ref{sec:arbiterA} compare \emph{operators} --- by their action on generic
fields --- rather than expressions, and why they are accompanied by a negative
control that corrupts precisely this ordering.

\section{\texorpdfstring{$\Aop$}{A} against the thesis, and against the
published operator form}\label{sec:against}

One object is used here and not derived in this note: the thesis system matrix
\cite{nestor1996}.  The published matrix--vector operator form \cite{mvwe} is
\emph{not} a second source.  It contains nothing beyond the thesis --- the two
are the same object under one constant similarity transform, as
\eqref{eq:similarity} shows entry by entry --- and it is used here for the one
thing an independent publication of the same result is good for: corroboration.
Two derivations made separately and agreeing is evidence; it is not a
contribution, and nothing in this note rests on it.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{A note on where this starts.}  An earlier version of this note presented
the published operator matrices as the foundation and the thesis system matrix
as an arbiter for them.  That is the wrong way round.  The thesis carries
$\bA(k_x,k_y)$ in \S2.1, equation~(Akdef), and the purpose of this work is to
extend and test the thesis, not to check the thesis against a later paper.  What
the operator form genuinely contributes is narrower: the thesis has the system
only in the laterally \emph{invariant} case, where $\partial_\alpha \to ik_\alpha$
has already been taken, and a cube requires the coefficients to vary laterally.
So the operator form is a \emph{generalisation} of (Akdef), and it is
that generalisation --- not the system --- that is new here.  The consequences
of getting this backwards were not only presentational; they are collected in
\S\ref{sec:thesisspectral}.
\end{quote}

\subsection{Arbiter A: the published isotropic operator matrices}\label{sec:arbiterA}

The blocks \eqref{eq:A11A22}--\eqref{eq:A12A21} are transcribed separately and
compared against those built from \eqref{eq:Ablocks}.  The comparison is made by
acting on a vector of generic symbolic functions, so it tests the derivative
ordering as well as the coefficients.  All four match.

A check that cannot fail is worth nothing, so a corrupted $\Aop_{22}$ --- with
the derivative wrongly placed outside the modulus --- is also tested, and is
rejected.

\subsection{The anchor: the thesis system matrix
\texorpdfstring{(Akdef)}{(Akdef)}}\label{sec:arbiterB}

The thesis carries a $6\times6$ Fourier-domain system matrix $\bA(k_x,k_y)$ for
a laterally invariant medium, in a \emph{different} field vector
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{b} = (u_3,\,u_1,\,u_2,\,\tau_{33},\,\tau_{31},\,\tau_{32})\tp,
\end{equation}
i.e.\ displacement--stress rather than velocity--traction, ordered $(z,x,y)$
rather than $(1,2,3)$, and normalised so that $\rho\omega^2$ appears where the
paper has $i\omega\rho$.  It uses the shorthand
\begin{equation}
  \gamma = \frac{\lambda}{\lambda+2\mu},\quad
  a = \frac{1}{\lambda+2\mu},\quad
  b = \frac{1}{\mu},\quad
  \zeta = \frac{4\mu(\lambda+\mu)}{\lambda+2\mu},\quad
  \chi = \frac{2\mu\lambda}{\lambda+2\mu}.
\end{equation}
The thesis attaches a warning to these that must travel with them: the shorthand
$\gamma, a, b, \zeta, \chi$ is \emph{local to} (Akdef), and is \emph{not} the
$\gamma, \zeta, \chi$ of the List of Symbols, which denote the Chapter~5
material-contrast ratios.  Since this note uses $\zeta$ and $\chi$ throughout as
the in-plane stiffnesses that survive the elimination of $\varepsilon_{33}$, the
collision is worth stating once rather than inheriting silently.  With that
said, $\zeta - \chi \equiv 2\mu$, which is the thesis's own footnote and the
cleanest check on the pair.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Comparing \eqref{eq:nu} with these:} $\zeta \equiv \nu_1$ and
$\chi \equiv \nu_2$.  The thesis's in-plane stiffnesses and the paper's
auxiliary moduli are the same two quantities, born in the same elimination
\eqref{eq:U}.  This is verified, not asserted.
\end{quote}

Setting $\lambda,\mu,\rho$ constant and acting on $e^{i(k_1x_1+k_2x_2)}$ reduces
$\Aop$ to a matrix.  With the constant map $\mathbf{b} = \bE\bq$ built from
$\bu = \bv/(-i\omega)$, $\bta_3 = -\bq_{1:3}$ and the permutation
$(1,2,3)\to(3,1,2)$, the identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:similarity}
  \bA_{\text{thesis}} = \bE\,\bA_{\text{paper}}\,\bE^{-1}
\end{equation}
holds entry by entry.  Since the two matrices were derived independently and
published separately, this is evidence rather than bookkeeping.

\subsection{Structural checks}\label{sec:symrel}

Three further properties are verified on the reduced $6\times6$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item the operator symmetry relations $\Aop_{11}\tp = -\Aop_{22}$,
      $\Aop_{12}\tp = \Aop_{12}$, $\Aop_{21}\tp = \Aop_{21}$, where the
      transpose is the formal adjoint under integration by parts
      ($\partial_\alpha\tp = -\partial_\alpha$);
\item the quasi-Hamiltonian relation \eqref{eq:quasiham}, which in the
      wavenumber domain reads $\bA\tp(-\mathbf{k})\Jsix = -\Jsix\bA(\mathbf{k})$;
\item the characteristic polynomial factorises as
      $(s^2 + k_{z,P}^2)(s^2 + k_{z,S}^2)^2$, recovering
      $\alpha^2 = (\lambda+2\mu)/\rho$ and $\beta^2 = \mu/\rho$ from a
      construction in which no wave speed was ever written down.
\end{enumerate}
Item 3 is the one that binds the algebra to physics: nothing upstream knows the
wave speeds.

\section{The contrast operator}

\subsection{Definition and the Lippmann--Schwinger equation}

Inside the scatterer the medium is $(\lambda+\Delta\lambda,\ \mu+\Delta\mu,\
\rho+\Delta\rho)$.  Writing $\Aop^{\text{tot}} = \Aop + \dA$, the total field
solves the background system driven by its own contrast,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:LS}
  (\partial_3 - \Aop)\,\bq = \dA\,\bq + \bd,
\end{equation}
which is the Lippmann--Schwinger equation \cite{lippmann1950} of the first-order system.

\subsection{The foundation check}

Before \eqref{eq:LS} means anything, $\bq$ must actually satisfy
\eqref{eq:mvwe}.  Building $\bq$ from a \emph{generic} displacement field in a
\emph{laterally varying} medium and forming $\partial_3\bq - \Aop\bq$ gives two
halves with different strengths:
\begin{itemize}
\item rows $4$--$6$ vanish \textbf{identically}, for any $\bu$ whatever.  They
      are the constitutive relation rearranged; no equation of motion is used.
      Failure here would mean the elimination \eqref{eq:U} is wrong.
\item rows $1$--$3$ equal \textbf{exactly} the Navier body force
      $f_i = -\rho\omega^2 u_i - \partial_j\tau_{ij}$.  Anything left over would
      be a defect in $\Aop_{11}$ or $\Aop_{12}$.
\end{itemize}
Both hold.  This is a considerably stronger statement than the plane-wave and
eigenvalue checks of \S\ref{sec:symrel}, because $\bu$ is generic and the
moduli vary laterally, so every ordering term in \eqref{eq:A11A22} is exercised.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{$\dA$}{DeltaA} is not linear in the contrast}
\label{sec:nonlinear}

The Navier potential is exactly linear in $(\Delta\lambda,\Delta\mu,\Delta\rho)$.
$\dA$ is not, because the elimination inverted $\bC_{33}$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:resum}
  (\dA_{21})_{11} = i\omega\left(\frac{1}{\mu+\Delta\mu} - \frac{1}{\mu}\right)
  = -\,\frac{i\omega\,\Delta\mu}{\mu(\mu+\Delta\mu)},
\end{equation}
whose ratio to its linearisation $-i\omega\Delta\mu/\mu^2$ is exactly
$\mu/(\mu+\Delta\mu)$.  At the project's validated moderate contrast
($\alpha = 5$, $\beta = 3$, $\rho = 2.5$, so $\lambda = 17.5$, $\mu = 22.5$ GPa;
$\Delta\lambda = 2$, $\Delta\mu = 1$ GPa):
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lr}
\toprule
quantity & value\\
\midrule
$\Delta\mu/\mu$ & $0.04444$\\
exact\,/\,linear for $\Delta b = \Delta(1/\mu)$ & $0.95745$\\
exact\,/\,linear for $\Delta a = \Delta(1/K_c)$ & $0.93985$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The first-order form has already summed part of the local response exactly.
\S\ref{sec:whatitbuys} measures what that is worth, and finds the answer is not
the obvious one.

\section{The augmentation}

\subsection{Why a derivative must be moved}

Inside the scatterer the contrasts are constant, so every coefficient of $\dA$
carries the indicator $\mathbf{1}_V$.  A derivative standing to the \emph{left}
of such a coefficient differentiates the indicator and produces a surface delta
on the cube faces.  Integration by parts over all space moves that derivative
onto the test function instead, where it meets something smooth:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:byparts}
  \int \phi_i\,\bigl(-\partial_\alpha( m\,\mathbf{1}_V\,\psi_j)\bigr)\,d\mathbf{x}
  = +\int (\partial_\alpha\phi_i)\, m\,\mathbf{1}_V\,\psi_j\,d\mathbf{x},
\end{equation}
and the face term never arises.  This is the step the thesis describes as
transferring the derivatives onto the source and receiver coordinates of the
propagator, and it is what converts a singular integro-differential equation
into a nonsingular, compact integral equation.

\subsection{The augmented dimension}

After the transfer every term has the form
$(\partial^{a}\phi)\tp\,\cdot\,\text{const}\,\cdot\,(\partial^{b}\psi)$ with
$a,b\in\{0,1\}$, so the form is \emph{multiplicative} on the enlarged vector
$\mathcal{L}\psi = (\psi,\ \partial_1\psi,\ \partial_2\psi)$ --- nominally
$6+6+6 = 18$ slots.  The true dimension is smaller and is counted, not assumed.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Result.}  Exactly $10 = 6+4$ slots are used.  The four extra are
$\partial_1 v_1,\ \partial_2 v_1,\ \partial_1 v_2,\ \partial_2 v_2$: the lateral
gradients of the two \emph{horizontal} velocity components, and nothing else.
\end{quote}

Setting $\partial_2 \to ik_y$, as a $2\tfrac12$-D formulation does, makes two of
those four multiplicative, leaving $6+2 = 8$.  That is precisely the thesis's
count for its augmented coupling matrix, reproduced here from an independent
$3$-D Cartesian derivation.  The agreement is a corroboration of the thesis
construction, not a restatement of it.

The transfer identity is verified three ways: the augmented matrix is constant
(no derivative operators survive); the augmented form reproduces the transferred
bilinear; and the untransferred and transferred bilinears differ by an
explicitly exhibited lateral divergence $\partial_\alpha P_\alpha$.

\section{The test space, derived}\label{sec:testspace}

\subsection{The argument}

At this point there are ten augmented slots and, for an affine trial space, nine
parameters.  The temptation is to choose nine conditions.  That temptation must
be resisted: a subset chosen by scoring against the arbiter it is later judged
by is a fitted parameter wearing a derivation's clothes.

There is no choice to make.  $\Jsix$ is \emph{anti}symmetric, and
\eqref{eq:quasiham} applies to $\dA$ as well as to $\Aop$ (it is linear in the
operator), so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:symmetric}
  (\Jsix\dA)\tp = \dA\tp\Jsix\tp = \dA\tp(-\Jsix) = \Jsix\dA .
\end{equation}
$\Jsix\dA$ is a \textbf{symmetric} matrix, so
\begin{equation}
  b[\phi,\psi] = \phi\tp\,\Jsix\dA\,\psi
\end{equation}
is a symmetric bilinear form.  A symmetric form admits exactly one natural
projection:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:testspace}
  \boxed{\ \text{test space} \;=\; \Jsix \times \text{trial space}. \ }
\end{equation}
Verified, together with the negative control: drop $\Jsix$ and the symmetry is
lost, both at operator level and in the projected system.

\subsection{The ten-versus-nine mismatch dissolves}

The ten slots were never test conditions.  They measure how many derivative
channels the \emph{form} touches --- its representation --- not how many
independent functionals it supplies.  Each trial function $\psi_k$ gives exactly
one test functional $(\Jsix\psi_k)\tp$, so the number of conditions equals the
trial dimension by construction.

\subsection{The effective coupling matrix}

Collecting the last three subsections gives the object that goes inside the
integral.  The form $b[\phi,\psi] = \phi\tp\Jsix\dA\,\psi$ became
\emph{multiplicative} on the augmented vector $\mathcal{L}\psi$ once the
derivatives were transferred, so it has a matrix there; call it $\dC$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dCdef}
  b[\phi,\psi] = (\mathcal{L}\phi)\tp\,\dC\,(\mathcal{L}\psi)
  \quad\text{pointwise},
\end{equation}
the \emph{augmented contrast matrix}.  It is not a stiffness contrast and not
$\dA$: it is $\Jsix\dA$ rewritten on the enlarged vector, so it is symmetric by
\eqref{eq:symmetric}, it carries no derivatives of its own --- they are all in
$\mathcal{L}$ --- and by the count above only $10 = 6+4$ of the nominal $18$
slots of $\mathcal{L}\psi$ are occupied.  It is distinct from the Navier
coupling $\dCnav$ of \S\ref{sec:units}, which is the object the two routes are
later compared \emph{against}, not a name for this one.

For the affine trial space --- nine parameters, three rigid translations and six
uniform strains --- the projected coupling is then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dCeff}
  (\dCeff)_{kl} = \int_V (\mathcal{L}\psi_k)\tp\,\dC\,(\mathcal{L}\psi_l)\,dV
\end{equation}
is the $3$-D Cartesian analogue of the thesis's effective coupling matrix.  It
is $9\times9$, symmetric, of full rank $9$, vanishes with the contrast, and
--- because the trial functions are polynomials --- requires \textbf{no Green's
function at all}: the cube integral is exact.

The rigid-translation block is diagonal,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rigid}
  (\dCeff)_{1:3,1:3} = i\omega^{3}\,\Delta\rho\,V\,\bI_3,
  \qquad V = (2a)^3 .
\end{equation}

\paragraph{The power of $\omega$ is a basis statement, not a physical one.}
$\dA$'s density entry is $i\omega\Delta\rho$ acting on the velocity slot; the
trial carries $\bv = -i\omega\bu$ there; and $\Jsix$ pulls the test function's
velocity slot into the traction rows, supplying a second $-i\omega$.  Hence
$i\omega\cdot(-i\omega)\cdot(-i\omega) = i\omega^{3}$.  The displacement-basis
answer $i\omega\Delta\rho V$ is wrong by $\omega^2$.  This is the
displacement-versus-flux confusion in a new place, and the check asserts the
correct factor \emph{and} asserts the incorrect one is incorrect, so it cannot
drift back.

\section{What the first-order coupling actually buys}\label{sec:whatitbuys}

\subsection{The trial spaces coincide}

It is tempting to attribute any difference from the Navier formulation to the
nonlinearity of \S\ref{sec:nonlinear}, or to a different ansatz.  Neither is
right.  Inside the scatterer the moduli are constant, so
$\bta_3 = \bC_{33}^{\text{tot}}\,\boldsymbol\varepsilon_{i3}$ is a constant
\emph{invertible} map.  A uniform strain gives a uniform $\bta_3$ and an affine
$\bv$, and conversely: ``$\boldsymbol\varepsilon$ uniform'' and ``$\bta_3$
uniform plus in-plane $\boldsymbol\varepsilon$ uniform'' describe the same
nine-parameter family.

The two formulations are therefore \textbf{different projections of the same
residual onto the same trial space}.  The difference lives in the test
conditions.

\subsection{The density channel cannot differ}

With $\Delta\lambda = \Delta\mu = 0$, $\dA$ is exactly $i\omega\Delta\rho$ in
rows $1$--$3$, columns $4$--$6$, and reaches \emph{no} part of the stress-glut
slot: a density contrast is a pure body force.  The propagator's body-force
velocity block is $-i\omega\bG$, so both routes return
\begin{equation}\label{eq:amplification}
  A_u = \frac{1}{1 - \omega^2\Delta\rho\,\Gamma_0},
  \qquad \Gamma_0 = \int_V \bG\,dV,
\end{equation}
identically.  The modulus channels are the only place the two can part.

\subsection{The units the two couplings are written in}\label{sec:units}

The statement just made is about the \emph{amplification factor}, which is a
ratio, and ratios are where this comparison is safe.  The couplings themselves
are not in the same units, and it is worth saying so plainly because every other
check in this note is also a ratio, and none of them can see it.

The $\Jsix$ pairing tests against the field $q$, whose velocity slots carry
$v = -i\omega u$.  The Navier weak form, and this package's local $T$-matrix,
test against the \emph{displacement}.  So
\begin{equation}\label{eq:units}
  \dCeff = i\omega\,\times\,\bigl[\text{the same object in displacement units}\bigr],
  \qquad
  \dCeff\big|_{\text{rigid}} = i\omega^3\Delta\rho V
  \;\;\text{against}\;\;
  \omega^2\Delta\rho V .
\end{equation}
This is a change of units and not of physics.  It cancels out of every
amplification factor, every channel ratio and every Born limit expressed as a
ratio; it does \emph{not} cancel the moment $\dCeff$ is combined with an object
built in displacement units.  The $i\omega$ appearing in \eqref{eq:gapfactor} is
this same factor seen from the other side.

The check that establishes it is necessarily absolute rather than relative:
after dividing by $i\omega$, the residual $\|\dCeff/i\omega - \dCnav\|/\|\dCnav\|$
falls as $4.19\times10^{-2}$, $4.19\times10^{-3}$, $4.19\times10^{-4}$,
$4.19\times10^{-5}$ under decade reductions of the contrast --- the \emph{first}
power, so the residual is the genuine $O(\Delta c^2)$ difference of
\S\ref{sec:whatitbuys} against an $O(\Delta c)$ coupling, and not a second,
smaller units error hiding beneath the first.

\subsection{Born agreement, and an \texorpdfstring{$O(\Delta c^2)$}{O(Dc2)} difference}

Reading a proportionality constant off the rigid block alone gives $i\omega$,
and testing it on the remaining eighty entries \textbf{refutes} the hypothesis
$\dCeff = i\omega\,\dC_{\text{Navier}}$.  The two couplings agree exactly at
$O(\Delta c^{0})$ and $O(\Delta c^{1})$ --- identical Born limits, so nothing is
broken --- and differ from second order.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Where.}  Eleven of eighty-one entries differ.  With the parameter order
\[
  (c_1,c_2,c_3,\ e_{11},e_{22},e_{33},\ e_{12},e_{13},e_{23})
\]
the nonzero block is $\{e_{11},e_{22},e_{33}\}^{2}$ together with
$(e_{13},e_{13})$ and $(e_{23},e_{23})$.
\end{quote}

That is exactly the set of strains $\bta_3$ sees:
\begin{equation}
  \tau_{13} = 2\mu\,e_{13},\qquad
  \tau_{23} = 2\mu\,e_{23},\qquad
  \tau_{33} = \lambda(e_{11}+e_{22}+e_{33}) + 2\mu\,e_{33}.
\end{equation}
The in-plane shear $e_{12}$ is \textbf{untouched}, as it must be: $\tau_{12}$ was
eliminated, not resummed.  This was a falsifiable prediction about which entries
may be nonzero, and it held.  A representative entry is
\begin{equation}
  \bigl(\dCeff - i\omega\,\dC_{\text{Navier}}\bigr)_{e_{11}e_{11}}
  = -\,\frac{i\omega V\,\Delta\lambda^{2}}{\lambda+2\mu},
\end{equation}
pure second order, divided by $K_c$: the remainder of the geometric series for
$1/(K_c+\Delta K_c)$.

\subsection{Half of it is a broken symmetry}\label{sec:oh}

An isotropic cube in an isotropic whole space has an $\Oh$-symmetric
$T$-matrix; no direction is special.  But the first-order system is a recursion
in \emph{depth}: it eliminated $\tau_1,\tau_2$ and kept $\tau_3$, so $x_3$ is
preferred by construction.  The tell is already above --- $(e_{13},e_{13})$ and
$(e_{23},e_{23})$ differ while $(e_{12},e_{12})$ does not, and those three are
$\Oh$-equivalent.

Applying the cyclic element $x\to y\to z\to x$ (positive controls: $P^3 = \bI$,
and the Navier coupling \emph{is} invariant):

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
contrast (GPa) & invariant & breaking & ratio\\
\midrule
pure shear, $\Delta\mu = 1$                       & $2.62\times10^{-2}$ & $1.94\times10^{-2}$ & $1.3520$\\
moderate, $(2,\,1,\,0.1)$                         & $5.17\times10^{-4}$ & $2.93\times10^{-4}$ & $1.7669$\\

So the difference is neither pure artefact nor pure physics.  It carries both a
genuine $\Oh$-invariant resummation the Navier form lacks, and a spurious
preference for $x_3$.

\paragraph{A caveat on this test.}  The group used is the cyclic subgroup of
order $3$, not all $48$ elements of $\Oh$.  Invariance under it is necessary for
$\Oh$ invariance, not sufficient.

\subsection{What follows, and what does not}

Two readings are available and the measurement above does not separate them.

\emph{Against the route:} an approximation that breaks a symmetry the exact
answer possesses has introduced an error that no propagator can remove.

\emph{For the route:} the correction is not scattered.  It lands exactly on the
traction channel, treating $\bC_{33}$ exactly instead of leaving it to the
Neumann series, and leaves the in-plane channel alone.  Vertical $P$ incidence
excites $e_{33}$; vertical $S$ incidence excites $e_{13}$ or $e_{23}$; both are
corrected.  The in-plane shear, which the side-scattered field carries, is
unchanged.  If the response is dominated by reflection and transmission, the
asymmetry is ``$z$ treated better'' rather than ``$z$ treated worse''.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Consequence for any comparison.}  Do not score the raw coupling against
an independent arbiter in a homogeneous background.  Three effects are in play
at comparable magnitude --- the Galerkin-versus-collocation projection, the
$\Oh$-invariant resummation, and the artefact --- and a single number separates
none of them.  Any comparison must be channel-resolved.
\end{quote}

Whether the resummation is an \emph{improvement} is not established here.  The
local inversion $1/(K_c+\Delta K_c)$ is exact; whether that makes the closed
$T$-matrix better depends on how the ansatz error and the series truncation
interact, and those do not simply add.

\section{The propagator, and the bridge to it}\label{sec:bridge}

\subsection{It already exists}

Everything downstream needs the Green's matrix of \eqref{eq:mvwe}.  Write it
$\boldsymbol\Gamma$, defined by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gammadef}
  \bigl[\partial_z - \bA(\mathbf{k})\bigr]\,
  \boldsymbol\Gamma(\mathbf{k}; z,z') = \Isix\,\delta(z-z'),
\end{equation}
together with the radiation condition at $z\to\pm\infty$.  It is a $6\times6$
matrix in the state-vector basis of \eqref{eq:qdef}, not a Green's
\emph{tensor}: its $j$th column is the state vector produced at $z$ by a unit
jump in the $j$th component of $\bq$ imposed at $z'$, so it carries the
displacement-to-displacement, traction-to-displacement and
traction-to-traction responses together.  Three consequences are used
repeatedly and are worth naming here rather than where each is first needed.
\eqref{eq:gammadef} makes $\boldsymbol\Gamma$ satisfy the \emph{homogeneous}
system in $z$ away from the source plane, which is \eqref{eq:bridge} below; it
fixes the jump across $z=z'$ to be exactly $\Isix$, which
\S\ref{sec:layered} measures and which \S\ref{sec:leibniz} shows is what makes
the integral equation and the differential equation the same statement; and,
with the radiation condition, it makes $\boldsymbol\Gamma$ one-sided --- built
from downgoing modes below the source and upgoing modes above --- which is what
\eqref{eq:recip} tests and what \S\ref{sec:layered} uses to name the difference
between a layered and a whole-space background.

One warning about the symbol.  $\boldsymbol\Gamma$ here is unrelated to the
scalar $\Gamma_0 = \int_V \bG\,dV$ of \eqref{eq:amplification}, which is a
volume moment of the Kelvin tensor and not this object evaluated anywhere.  The
two are kept apart by weight and by subscript only, so the bold face is
load-bearing.

It does not need to be built: the stratified $6\times6$ Green's function and its
validated wrapper already return it, in the basis
$(u_z,u_x,u_y,T_{zz},T_{xz},T_{yz})$ --- which is exactly the basis the thesis
writes $\bA$ in.  That the two are the same object had, however, been
\emph{assumed}.

\subsection{The bridge identity}

Away from the source plane the Green's matrix solves the homogeneous system in
its receiver coordinate, so for two receiver depths in one uniform layer
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bridge}
  \boldsymbol\Gamma(z_2,z') = \exp\!\bigl(\bA\,(z_2-z_1)\bigr)\,\boldsymbol\Gamma(z_1,z'),
\end{equation}
with no free parameters.  The identity is \emph{local}: it holds however
complicated the medium between the source and $z_1$ is, because the ODE knows
only the layer the receiver sits in.  The gate therefore places the source above
a fast slab and the receivers below it, so transmission, reflection, conversion
and interbed multiples are all present in $\boldsymbol\Gamma$ and
\eqref{eq:bridge} must still hold exactly.

\subsection{Reporting residuals against the arithmetic floor}

A bare relative residual is not comparable across incidence angles.  Beyond the
critical slowness the propagator carries both a growing and a decaying
evanescent branch, so $\exp(\bA\,dz)$ has dynamic range $\sim e^{|k|dz}$ ---
about $1.6\times10^{7}$ at the widest angle used.  Forming
$\exp(\bA\,dz)\,\boldsymbol\Gamma(z_1)$ then amplifies the rounding already
present by $\|\exp(\bA\,dz)\|$, and no correct implementation can beat
\begin{equation}
  \text{floor} = \epsilon\;\|\exp(\bA\,dz)\|\;
  \frac{\|\boldsymbol\Gamma(z_1)\|}{\|\boldsymbol\Gamma(z_2)\|}.
\end{equation}
Residuals below are quoted in units of that floor, so $O(1)$ means the identity
holds to the last bit available at that conditioning.

\section{The single-site \texorpdfstring{$T$}{T}-matrix}\label{sec:tmatrix}

This is the object the construction was for.  On the nine-parameter affine
trial space,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:T}
  \boxed{\ \mathbf{T} = \dCeff\bigl(\bI_9 - \mathcal{G}\,\dCeff\bigr)^{-1}, \ }
\end{equation}
with $\dCeff$ the projected coupling \eqref{eq:dCeff} and $\mathcal{G}$ of \eqref{eq:Gmoment}
the self propagator moment of the cube.

\subsection{What is new and what is borrowed}

New is $\dCeff$.  Borrowed is $\mathcal{G}$: the first-order route changes the
\emph{coupling}, not the Green's function.  The two moments it needs are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:selfmoments}
  \bG_{ij} = \int_V G^0_{ij}\,dV,
  \qquad
  M_{in,pk} = \int_V \partial'_p\partial'_k G^0_{in}\,dV .
\end{equation}

These are \emph{derived} in \S\ref{sec:moments} below, not taken from a table.
In the parameter basis
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Gmoment}
  \mathcal{G} = \frac{1}{V}
  \begin{pmatrix}\Gamma_0\,\bI_3 & \mathbf{0}\\ \mathbf{0} & -\bS\,\bW^{-1}\end{pmatrix},
  \qquad \bW = \operatorname{diag}(1,1,1,2,2,2),
\end{equation}
where $\bS$ is the second moment $M$ of \eqref{eq:selfmoments} carried into the
$6\times6$ Voigt basis and symmetrised on its outer index pair,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Sdef}
  S_{mn,jk} = \tfrac12\bigl(M_{mj,kn} + M_{nj,km}\bigr),
  \qquad
  M_{in,pk} = \int_V \partial'_p\partial'_k G^0_{in}\,dV ,
\end{equation}
so that $\bS$ maps a uniform strain imposed on the cube to the response the
cube's \emph{own} radiated field produces back inside it, averaged over the
cube.  That self-response is what is called the \textbf{depolarisation} here and
at the two places below where the term recurs: the name is the standard one from
inclusion theory, where for an ellipsoid the corresponding object is Eshelby's
tensor \cite{eshelby1957}.  It is the object the $r=0$ delta of \S\ref{sec:moments} belongs to, and
the reason that delta cannot be dropped --- omitting it reverses the sign of
$\bS$, which is the failure \S\ref{sec:moments} and \S\ref{sec:triseries} both
warn about.  The symmetrisation is not cosmetic and is not
supplied by any symmetry $M$ already has.  $M$ is symmetric in
$p\leftrightarrow k$ because the derivatives commute, and in
$i\leftrightarrow n$ because $G^0$ is; but $m$ and $n$ in \eqref{eq:Sdef} sit in
\emph{different slots} --- $m$ is a Green's index in the first term and a
derivative index in the second --- so nothing makes $\bS$ symmetric on its outer
pair except writing it that way.  $\bW$ undoes the engineering doubling of the
shear slots.

$\bS$ here is not the $\bE$ of \eqref{eq:similarity}, nor the scaling
$\bSig$ of \eqref{eq:scaling}, nor the convention factors $\bS_u,\bS_d$ of
\eqref{eq:convention}; earlier drafts wrote all four with the same letter and
defined only two of them.

Note that \eqref{eq:Gmoment} is asserted, not derived --- which is exactly why
\S\ref{sec:calibration} exists.

\subsection{The self moments, derived}\label{sec:moments}

The integrand of $M$ goes as $1/r^3$, which is not absolutely integrable in
three dimensions.  The customary remedy is to quote a tabulated Eshelby tensor \cite{eshelby1957,mura1987}
and add a delta-function term at $r=0$ by hand; omit the delta and the
depolarisation comes out with the \emph{wrong sign}.  Nothing below is
tabulated and nothing is added by hand.

\subsubsection{The static Green's tensor}

In the static limit the elastodynamic tensor reduces to Kelvin's,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kelvin}
  G^0_{in}(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{a_0\,\delta_{in}}{r} + \frac{b_0\,x_ix_n}{r^3},
  \qquad
  a_0 = \frac{1}{8\pi}\Bigl(\frac1\mu + \frac{1}{\lambda+2\mu}\Bigr),
  \quad
  b_0 = \frac{1}{8\pi}\Bigl(\frac1\mu - \frac{1}{\lambda+2\mu}\Bigr),
\end{equation}
the coefficients being fixed by requiring that \eqref{eq:kelvin} satisfy the
Navier operator away from the origin.  An equivalent form makes the
distributional content visible, since $\partial_i\partial_n r = \delta_{in}/r -
x_ix_n/r^3$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kelvinhc}
  G^0_{in} = \frac{1}{4\pi\mu}\Bigl[\frac{\delta_{in}}{r}
             + h_c\,\partial_i\partial_n r\Bigr],
  \qquad h_c = -\frac{\lambda+\mu}{2(\lambda+2\mu)} ,
\end{equation}
and $a_0 = (1+h_c)/4\pi\mu$, $b_0 = -h_c/4\pi\mu$ identically.

\subsubsection{The moment as a distribution, and why no delta is inserted}

Write the moment as a pairing against the test function $w\,\mathbf{1}_V$
rather than as an integral of a function.  With $T = \partial_{\text{rest}}r^m$
and $\partial_q\mathbf{1}_V = -n_q\,\delta_{\partial V}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:recursion}
  \langle \partial_q T,\; w\,\mathbf{1}_V\rangle
  = \oint_{\partial V} n_q\,w\,T\,dA
  \;-\; \langle T,\; (\partial_q w)\,\mathbf{1}_V\rangle .
\end{equation}
That is the entire construction.  Two features make it exact rather than merely
plausible:

\begin{itemize}
\item $T$ is only ever evaluated \emph{on the boundary}, at distance $\Delta/2$
      from the origin, where every derivative of $r^m$ is smooth and bounded.
      The singular pointwise structure of $\partial^Dr^m$ at the origin is never
      formed, so there is no principal value to take and no delta to insert.
\item the recursion strictly lowers both the derivative count and the weight
      degree, terminating at $\int_V x_w\,r^m\,dV$, an ordinary convergent
      integral for $m\ge-1$.
\end{itemize}

The delta term is therefore neither added nor droppable --- it is already inside
the surface integral.  The sharp test is the Laplacian sum rule
$\sum_p E[-1;\{p,p\}] = -4\pi$, which a spherical excision returns as $0$.

\subsubsection{The face integrals, and one of them by pure geometry}

On the face $x_3 = \Delta/2$, scaling the in-face coordinates by $\Delta/2$
reduces every surface term to one of three integrals over $[-1,1]^2$:
\begin{align}\label{eq:faceints}
  j_1 &= \iint\frac{du\,dv}{(1+u^2+v^2)^{3/2}}
       &&= \frac{2\pi}{3}, \notag\\[2pt]
  j_2 &= \iint\frac{u^2\,du\,dv}{(1+u^2+v^2)^{5/2}}
       &&= -\frac{2(\sqrt3-\pi)}{9}, \\[2pt]
  k_1 &= \iint\frac{du\,dv}{(1+u^2+v^2)^{5/2}}
       &&= \frac{2(2\sqrt3+\pi)}{9}. \notag
\end{align}
All three are evaluated in closed form.  The first needs no integration at all:
$j_1$ \emph{is} the solid angle subtended by one face at the cube's centre, and
the six faces subtend $4\pi$, so $j_1 = 4\pi/6 = 2\pi/3$.  That identity is a
check on the scaling and orientation of \eqref{eq:faceints}, obtained
independently of any quadrature.

The zeroth moment needs one further integral, which also has a closed form:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ivr}
  \int_{[-1,1]^3}\frac{dV}{r}
  = -2\bigl(\pi + \log 64 - 12\log(1+\sqrt3)\bigr)
  = \tfrac43\log\bigl(70226 + 40545\sqrt3\bigr) - 2\pi
  = 9.520309455918214\ldots
\end{equation}
The two closed forms are independent evaluations of the same constant and agree
to the precision carried; the second is the one this package uses elsewhere.

\subsubsection{The closed forms}

Assembling \eqref{eq:kelvin} through the recursion \eqref{eq:recursion} with the
face integrals \eqref{eq:faceints} gives, with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Mdecomp}
  M_{in,pk} = A\,\delta_{in}\delta_{pk}
            + B\,(\delta_{ip}\delta_{nk} + \delta_{ik}\delta_{np})
            + C\,E_{inpk},
  \qquad
  E_{inpk} = \begin{cases}1 & i=n=p=k\\ 0 & \text{otherwise,}\end{cases}
\end{equation}
the third structure being the one a cubic --- as opposed to isotropic ---
geometry admits,
\begin{align}
  B &= \frac{\sqrt3\,(\lambda+\mu)}{6\pi\mu(\lambda+2\mu)}, \label{eq:Bclosed}\\
  A &= B - \frac{1}{3\mu}, \label{eq:Aclosed}\\
  C &= -\Bigl(5 - \frac{2\pi}{\sqrt3}\Bigr)B, \label{eq:Cclosed}\\
  G_{ij} &= \delta_{ij}\,\Bigl(a_0 + \frac{b_0}{3}\Bigr)\Bigl(\frac{\Delta}{2}\Bigr)^{\!2}
            \int_{[-1,1]^3}\frac{dV}{r}. \label{eq:Gclosed}
\end{align}
$A$, $B$ and $C$ are scale-free; $G$ carries $\Delta^2$.  Note that $A$, $B$ and
$C$ are \emph{not} three independent constants --- the whole rank-four moment is
carried by $B$ together with the elementary $1/3\mu$, which is a structural
statement the tabulated presentation obscures.  $G_{ij}$ must be a multiple of
$\delta_{ij}$, because a rank-two cubic invariant has no other form: the cube
cannot distinguish its own axes at that order.

  \sum_p M_{in,pp}
  = -\,\frac{\delta_{in}}{\mu}\Bigl(1 + \frac{2h_c}{3}\Bigr)
  = -\,\delta_{in}\,\frac{2\lambda+5\mu}{3\mu(\lambda+2\mu)} ,

derived without reference to the recursion at all.  The assembled moment

present and correctly weighted.

\paragraph{What the split does and does not license.}  It is tempting to read
this as the first-order $T$ failing an external test while the ordinary route
passes.  That reading claims more than the check can deliver, and the two halves
must be kept apart.

\emph{What it gives.}  An \textbf{arbiter-free lower bound on the error of the
first-order $T$}.  If $A(e_{12})$ and $A(e_{13})$ must be equal and differ by
$\delta$, then whichever lies closer to the truth, the larger of the two errors
is at least $\delta/2 \approx 4.2\times10^{-4}$.  An absolute error estimate
obtained from a symmetry alone, with nothing external to compare against, is
worth having --- and it is the absolute companion this project requires
alongside any ratio test.

\emph{What it does not give.}  Any bound whatever on the ordinary route's error.
A symmetric approximation can be symmetrically wrong, missing the truth in
\emph{both} channels by more than $8.4\times10^{-4}$ while satisfying the
symmetry exactly.  Symmetry compliance is not accuracy.  Setting a bound on one
formulation against no bound on the other is not a comparison, and no ordering
of the two follows from this table.

\emph{What is nonetheless attributable.}  The breaking is not manufactured by
the projection mismatch of \S\ref{sec:tmatrix}: $\mathcal{G}$ is itself
$\Oh$-symmetric, and \S\ref{sec:oh} measured the coupling's asymmetry
symbolically with no propagator present at all.  The asymmetry is a property of
the coupling, and it survives into $\mathbf{T}$.

\paragraph{The channel set has to include $e_{13}$.}  The coupling leaves
$e_{12}$ untouched by construction, so a comparison spanning only $\theta$,
$e_{\text{diag}}$ and $e_{12}$ reports that the shear channels agree --- true,
and uninformative.  A channel-resolved comparison is only as good as the
channels chosen, and the $\Oh$-equivalent pair is the one that carries the
information.

\subsection{What this does and does not settle}

The Born limits agree: at a contrast of $10^{-6}$ the two couplings differ by
$4.2\times10^{-8}$, i.e.\ at $O(\Delta c)$ as they must.

\section{The propagator moment from the first-order system}\label{sec:propmom}

\S\ref{sec:tmatrix} took $\mathcal{G}$ from the ordinary Kelvin tensor, so the
$T$-matrix assembled there is first-order in its \emph{coupling} only.  This
section replaces that half.  The plan is
\texttt{docs/plans/2026-09-18-first-order-propagator-moment.md}; what follows is
written as each step lands, and says plainly where the front is.

The object is the sandwiched form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sandwich}
  \bigl[\dCeff\,\mathcal{G}\,\dCeff\bigr]_{kl}
  = \bigl\langle \Jsix\psi_k \bigm| \dA\,\boldsymbol\Gamma\,\dA \bigm| \psi_l \bigr\rangle .
\end{equation}
Plain Galerkin on $\bq$ is not available: its mass matrix
$\langle\Jsix\psi_k,\psi_l\rangle$ is antisymmetric, and an antisymmetric matrix
in \emph{odd} dimension --- here nine --- is singular.

\subsection{Step 1: \texorpdfstring{$\Gamma$}{Gamma}, built from
\texorpdfstring{$\Aop$}{A} alone}

Solving \eqref{eq:gammadef} by the spectral representation, the radiating
Green's matrix is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gammaspec}
  \boldsymbol\Gamma(z,z') =
  \begin{cases}
    +\bP_d\,e^{\bA(z-z')}, & z > z',\\[2pt]
    -\bP_u\,e^{\bA(z-z')}, & z < z',
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
with $\bP_d,\bP_u$ the spectral projectors onto the down- and up-going
eigenspaces, so that $\boldsymbol\Gamma$ decays away from the source in both
directions and jumps by $\bP_d + \bP_u = \bI$ across it.

\paragraph{Projectors, not eigenvectors.}  The thesis writes the eigen-matrix
column by column with normalisation factors $\epsilon_P,\epsilon_S,\epsilon_H$
left symbolic.  Those factors are fixed by a flux normalisation that degenerates
at normal incidence, where the horizontal direction no longer distinguishes the
$SV$ and $SH$ polarisations --- exactly the $k_\parallel\to0$ difficulty.  The
spectral projector
\begin{equation}\label{eq:projector}
  \bP_d = \bV_d\bigl(\bW_d\tp\bV_d\bigr)^{-1}\bW_d\tp
\end{equation}
is invariant under any rescaling of the columns of $\bV_d$, so building
$\boldsymbol\Gamma$ from projectors removes the ambiguity instead of carrying
it.  That invariance is verified directly, by rescaling every eigenvector by a
random complex factor and confirming $\bP_d$ is unchanged.

\paragraph{The matrix exponential must never be formed.}
Equation~\eqref{eq:gammaspec} is a statement, not an algorithm.  Evaluating it as
written --- projecting \emph{after} exponentiating --- builds the growing branch
and then cancels it.  At a separation of $640$\,km the growing entries reach
$e^{+28}\approx10^{12}$, so the cancellation leaves $10^{-4}$ of absolute noise
on a true answer of $3\times10^{-8}$.  This is the Thomson--Haskell instability \cite{thomson1950,haskell1953},
the reason invariant imbedding exists, reappearing inside our own construction;
it announces itself as a decay rate that turns \emph{negative} at large
separation.  Applying the exponential to the projected eigenvalues never forms
the growing branch at all,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gammastable}
  \boldsymbol\Gamma(z-z') = \pm\!\!\sum_{i\,\in\,\text{down/up}}\!\!
    e^{s_i(z-z')}\,\mathbf{v}_i\mathbf{w}_i\tp,
  \qquad \mathbf{w}_i\!\cdot\!\mathbf{v}_j = \delta_{ij},
\end{equation}
and is what is used.  The moment integral needs only $|z-z'|\le 2a$, where the
instability cannot bite; a layered background with thick layers is where it
would.

\paragraph{$\omega$ is complex from the outset.}  The vertical wavenumbers
$k_{z,c} = \sqrt{k_c^2-k^2}$ vanish on the circles $|k| = k_P$ and $|k| = k_S$,
which the lateral integral must cross, and $k_z$ sits in a denominator.  At real
$\omega$ a quadrature straddles an integrable $1/\sqrt{\cdot}$ singularity and
does not converge; the directional-sweep work measured $1.9\times10^{-1} \to
1.1\times10^{-1}$ over an eightfold refinement, non-monotone.  With damping
$0.02$ the smallest $|s|$ \emph{on} the $P$ circle is $0.240$ rather than zero.

\paragraph{Checks.}  Thirteen, all passing: the spectrum is
$\{\pm ik_{z,P}\}$ and $\{\pm ik_{z,S}\}$ twice; $\bP_d+\bP_u=\bI$, both
idempotent, both commuting with $\bA$; $\bP_d$ invariant under rescaling;
$\partial_z\boldsymbol\Gamma = \bA\boldsymbol\Gamma$ on both sides; the jump
exactly $\bI$; and the decay rate converging to the slowest eigenvalue,
$0.034990 \to 0.033901 \to 0.032857 \to 0.032436$ against $0.032393$ --- a rate,
not a bar, because at any one finite separation the faster mode still
contributes.

\subsection{Step 2: the vertical integral, in closed form}

The vertical integral is exact, but it is \emph{not} an integral over the
square.  The two halves of $\Gamma$ carry different matrices, so the double
integral splits over the two triangles and they cannot be recombined: treating
the kernel as a function of $|z-z'|$ alone would symmetrise the propagator and
destroy the up/down distinction the first-order system exists to carry.

With affine trial fields the weights are $1$ and $z$, so four triangular
integrals are needed,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tridef}
  T_{mn}(q) = \int_{-a}^{a}\!\!dz\int_{-a}^{z}\!\!dz'\;
              z^{m}z'^{n}\,e^{-q(z-z')},
\end{equation}
the lower triangle being $T_{nm}$ by relabelling.  All four are elementary:
\begin{align}
  T_{00} &= \frac{2aq - 1 + e^{-2aq}}{q^{2}}, \label{eq:T00}\\
  T_{10} &= -T_{01} = \frac{aq - 1 + (1+aq)\,e^{-2aq}}{q^{3}}, \label{eq:T10}\\
  T_{11} &= \frac{3 - 3(1+aq)^{2}e^{-2aq} + a^{2}q^{2}(2aq-3)}{3q^{4}}.
           \label{eq:T11}
\end{align}
Summing the two triangles with a common matrix recovers the familiar square
integral $2(2aq-1+e^{-2aq})/q^{2}$, and the odd weight cancels between them by
parity --- both checked, as is the large-$q$ behaviour $T_{00}\sim 2a/q$, each
triangle carrying half of the square's $4a/q$.

The decisive check of this step is not the closed forms themselves but the
\emph{assembly}: the vertical integration of $\Gamma$ performed through
\eqref{eq:T00}--\eqref{eq:T11}, matrices and all, reproduces a brute-force
double quadrature of $\Gamma$ to $2.5\times10^{-9}$ relative.

\subsubsection{The same integrals as a series, and why they are
needed}\label{sec:triseries}

\eqref{eq:T00}--\eqref{eq:T11} are exact and, evaluated as written, lose most of
their digits as $q\to0$.  Expanding \eqref{eq:T11} gives
\begin{equation}
  3 - 3(1+x)^{2}e^{-2x} + x^{2}(2x-3) = \tfrac{4}{5}x^{5} - \tfrac{2}{3}x^{6} +
  O(x^{7}), \qquad x = aq,
\end{equation}
so a numerator of order $x^{5}$ is assembled from terms of order unity: at
$x = 0.025$ about eight digits are gone.  That is not a corner of the parameter
space --- it is the whole propagating window $k < \omega/\beta$, which is
precisely where the radiation lives.

The remedy is the series.  Substituting $u = z-z'$ in \eqref{eq:tridef} and
expanding the exponential,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:triseries}
  T_{mn}(q) = \sum_{j\ge0} \frac{(-q)^{j}}{j!}
              \int_{0}^{2a}\!\! u^{j}\,J_{mn}(u)\,du ,
  \qquad
  J_{mn}(u) = \int_{-a}^{a-u} (z'+u)^{m} z'^{n}\,dz' ,
\end{equation}
and every $u$-integral is elementary.  With $b = 2a$,
\begin{equation}
  J_{00} = b - u, \quad
  J_{10} = -J_{01} = \tfrac{b}{2}u - \tfrac{u^{2}}{2}, \quad
  J_{11} = \tfrac{b^{3}}{12} - \tfrac{b^{2}}{4}u + \tfrac{u^{3}}{6},
\end{equation}
giving coefficients of $(-qb)^{j}/j!$ equal to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tricoef}
  \frac{b^{2}}{(j+1)(j+2)}, \qquad
  \frac{b^{3}}{2(j+2)(j+3)}, \qquad
  b^{4}\Bigl[\frac{1}{12(j+1)} - \frac{1}{4(j+2)} + \frac{1}{6(j+4)}\Bigr]
\end{equation}
for $(0,0)$, $(1,0)$ and $(1,1)$ respectively.  Two structural checks: at $q=0$
the first gives $2a^{2}$ and the second $2a^{3}/3$, which are
$\iint_{z>z'}1$ and $\iint_{z>z'}z$; the third gives \emph{zero}, and indeed
$\iint_{z>z'} zz' = 0$ by parity.  Its $j=1$ term is $\tfrac{4}{15}a^{5}q$, which
is exactly what \eqref{eq:T11} tends to, $\tfrac{4}{5}a^{5}q^{5}/3q^{4}$.  The
series is entire, so it is valid for the imaginary $q$ that propagating modes
carry; it is used below $|2aq| = 3$, where it is also the accurate branch.

\subsection{Step 3: the lateral integral}

These are \textbf{Sommerfeld-type integrals} --- integrals over horizontal
wavenumber, with the vertical dependence already reduced to the propagator, of
the kind that layered-media Green's functions have always been evaluated by.
Naming them so is not decoration: it says at once which family of numerical
difficulty this is, and that the difficulty is a known one with a known remedy
rather than something peculiar to the first-order formulation.  What is peculiar
to it is only \emph{why} the integrand fails to decay, which the power count
below settles.

Count powers as $|k|\to\infty$ for the \emph{untransferred} form, in which
$\partial_\alpha\to ik_\alpha$: the box form factor gives $k^{-2}$,
\eqref{eq:T00} gives $q^{-1}\sim|k|^{-1}$, the two contrast operators give
$k^{2}$, and the measure gives $k$.  The strain channel does not converge.
(The transferred form of \S\ref{sec:transferred} does; that is one of the things
the augmentation buys, and it is taken up there.)

The divergence is not an artefact to be trimmed.  A delta function in real space
is a constant in $k$-space, so the non-decaying tail of the integrand \emph{is}
the Eshelby term of \S\ref{sec:moments}.  A bare cutoff discards precisely the
piece whose omission reverses the sign of the depolarisation.  The remedy is to
split the integrand into a convergent remainder and an asymptote that is
integrated in closed form:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:splitsub}
  I(\mathbf{k}) = \bigl[I(\mathbf{k}) - I_\infty(\mathbf{k})\bigr] + I_\infty(\mathbf{k}),
\end{equation}
with the bracket integrated numerically --- convergent, and its convergence
measured as a rate --- and $\int I_\infty\,d^2k$ evaluated in closed form over
the whole plane, where the delta content resides.  The asymptote is taken of the
$z$-integrated kernel \eqref{eq:T00}--\eqref{eq:T11}, with the box form factors
kept exact: they are what makes the remainder converge, so expanding them would
defeat the subtraction.

\paragraph{Where the credit sits.}  Two different things are borrowed here and
neither is devised in this note.

The \emph{method} --- subtract the large-wavenumber asymptote of a slowly
convergent integrand, integrate the remainder numerically and the asymptote in
closed form --- is the standard remedy for slowly convergent Sommerfeld-type
integrals, and is standard in exactly this setting, wavenumber integration for
layered elastic media, where it is known as \textbf{static term subtraction}
\cite{hisada1994,hisada1995,luco1983}.  Hisada's case is the hardest one and is
also ours: when source and receiver approach the same depth the integrand ceases
to decay, and for equal depths the stress integrand \emph{grows}.  The self
moment of a cube has $z$ and $z'$ in the same body, so it sits at that limit by
construction.  The same technique is called \textbf{singularity extraction} in
the computational-electromagnetics literature on layered-media Green's functions
\cite{michalski1997}, where the subtracted piece is the quasi-static term.

The \emph{physics} that says which asymptote to subtract is Eshelby's
\cite{eshelby1957,mura1987}: the non-decaying tail is the delta at $r=0$, and
dropping it reverses the sign of the depolarisation.  This note takes that from
there rather than rediscovering it.

What is new is only the setting --- the horizontal wavenumber integrals the
first-order formulation mandates, in which the tail arises from the two contrast
operators rather than from a quoted inclusion tensor.  ``Split-and-subtract'' is
used below as a shorthand for that arrangement and is a label, not a citation.

\subsubsection{The scaling that makes the expansion uniform}

$\bA$ is not uniformly $O(k)$.  Rows $1$--$3$ carry $k$, but rows $5$--$6$ carry
$k^2$, because the basis mixes displacement with traction and the two have
different dimensions; expanding $\bA$ in $1/k$ as it stands mixes orders between
blocks.  The traction of an evanescent field scales as $\mu k u$, so the natural
balance is $\bq \to (\bu, \bta_3/k)$.  Under
$\bSig = \operatorname{diag}(1,1,1,k,k,k)$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:scaling}
  \widetilde{\bA} = \bSig^{-1}\bA\,\bSig
\end{equation}
is uniformly $O(k)$: the upper-right block gains a factor $k$, the lower-left
loses one, and the diagonal blocks are untouched.  Measured, $\|\bA\|/k$ grows
as $5.3\times10^{3} \to 5.3\times10^{5}$ over two decades in $k$ while
$\|\widetilde{\bA}\|/k$ settles at $53.0100 \to 53.0101$.  Being a similarity
transform it changes no eigenvalue, and $|s|/k \to 1$ for all six.

The scaling is also what makes the decomposition numerically usable.  On the
unscaled matrix the down- and up-going eigenvectors become nearly parallel as
$k$ grows --- the evanescent limit degenerates --- the eigen-decomposition is
reported badly conditioned, and the kernel entries collapse into rounding error,
from which no asymptotic power can be read.  The decomposition is therefore done
on $\widetilde{\bA}$ and mapped back, $\bK = \bSig\,\widetilde{\bK}\,\bSig^{-1}$,
checked against the verified kernel of step 2 at moderate $k$.

off numerically, over decades in $k$, rather than derived: a measured power is
harder to fool oneself with than one obtained from a $6\times6$
eigen-perturbation.  A dot marks an entry that vanishes, so no power can be read.

That block is also the one the physics predicts.  In the evanescent limit a

A traction response to a displacement that does not depend on $k$ \emph{is} a
local stiffness --- which is the Eshelby delta of \S\ref{sec:moments}, seen from
the first-order side.  The two representations identify the same object, and
$I_\infty$ has to reproduce exactly this block.

\subsubsection{The asymptote in closed form}

The limit of the non-decaying block is not merely numerical.  Writing
$\hat{\mathbf{k}}$ for the lateral direction,
\begin{align}
  C\bigl[\tau_{zz}\!\leftarrow\!u_z\bigr] &= -\nu_1
     = -\frac{4\mu(\lambda+\mu)}{\lambda+2\mu}, \label{eq:Cnu1}\\
  C\bigl[\tau_{\alpha z}\!\leftarrow\!u_\beta\bigr]
     &= \mu\bigl(\hat{k}_\alpha\hat{k}_\beta - \delta_{\alpha\beta}\bigr).
     \label{eq:Cinplane}
\end{align}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{$\nu_1$ is not a new constant.}  It is the same $\nu_1$ that appears in
$\Aop_{12}$ --- born in the elimination \eqref{eq:U}, and shown in
\S\ref{sec:arbiterB} to be the thesis's $\zeta$.  The subtraction is expressible
entirely in the operator's own constants.  A wrongly identified asymptote would
have no reason to land on $\nu_1$ and $\mu$.
\end{quote}

Both are established as \emph{rates}.  The relative residuals fall
$1.50\times10^{-3} \to 1.50\times10^{-4} \to 1.50\times10^{-5}$ across decades in
$k$, ratios $10.0006$ and $10.011$ --- clean $O(1/k)$, which is the $O(k_c/k)$
correction to the evanescent limit.  Agreement at a single $k$ would not
distinguish a correct closed form from a coincidence.

The in-plane form is the sharper test.  Two constants are fitted on \emph{two}
angles and then predict \emph{six}: $P = -22.4977$ and $Q = +22.4948$, both
equal to $\mu = 22.5$, with predictions tracking measurements to five digits
across $\theta\in[0,2.4]$.  The isotropic entry \eqref{eq:Cnu1} is constant to
seven digits over the same range.

\paragraph{What this settles about the integration.}  The asymptote \emph{is}
direction-dependent --- the relative variation over $\theta$ is $0.39$ --- so
$I_\infty$ is not a pure delta and the regulator cannot be avoided.  But the
structure is the most favourable available: $\delta_{\alpha\beta}$ and
$\hat{k}_\alpha\hat{k}_\beta$ are the only two rank-two invariants a direction
can build, and they transform to a local delta and a principal-value dipole
kernel respectively, both standard.  The $\tau_{zz}\!\leftarrow\!u_z$ entry is
isotropic and so is a pure delta of weight $-\nu_1$.

\subsubsection{The lateral integral, and where it must be evaluated}

The box form factor is separable --- the lateral transform of the cube's
indicator being
\[
  (2a)^2\operatorname{sinc}(k_1a)\,\operatorname{sinc}(k_2a) .
\]
With \eqref{eq:Cnu1}--\eqref{eq:Cinplane} and one lateral derivative from each
$\dA$, the integrand carries $k_ck_d$ against $C$, giving two families,
\[
  \int d^2k\;W\,k_ck_d
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  \int d^2k\;W\,k_ak_bk_ck_d/k^2 ,
\]
of which the first is separable and the second is not.  The elementary
one-dimensional integrals are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sincints}
  \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\operatorname{sinc}^2(ka)\,dk = \frac{\pi}{a},
  \qquad
  \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!k^2\operatorname{sinc}^2(ka)\,e^{-\epsilon|k|}dk
  = \frac{4}{4a^2\epsilon + \epsilon^3}
  = \frac{1}{a^2\epsilon} - \frac{\epsilon}{4a^4} .
\end{equation}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{The second is a pure pole with no finite part.}  So
$\int I_\infty\,d^2k$ is not a number, and the split-and-subtract cannot be
closed in $k$-space alone: adding back a pure pole restores exactly the
divergence it was introduced to remove.
\end{quote}

The method is not wrong; the tail is being evaluated in the wrong
representation.  Forming $|\widehat{S}|^2$ first and then integrating term by
term destroys the structure that makes the constant part finite.  By Parseval,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:parseval}
  \int\!\!\int f(\mathbf{x})\,K(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}')\,g(\mathbf{x}')
  = \int\!\frac{d^2k}{(2\pi)^2}\,\widehat{f}(-\mathbf{k})\,
      \widehat{K}(\mathbf{k})\,\widehat{g}(\mathbf{k}),
\end{equation}
so a $\widehat{K}$ tending to a constant $C$ contributes
$C\!\int_V\! f g\,d\mathbf{x}$ --- a \emph{local} term, finite, needing no
regulator at all.  This is verified directly in one dimension, both sides
returning $1/2a$.

The decomposition therefore splits by representation rather than by magnitude:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
piece & real-space object & evaluation\\
\midrule
isotropic, $-\nu_1$ and $P\delta_{\alpha\beta}$ & delta & local, elementary\\
traceless $\hat{k}_\alpha\hat{k}_\beta - \tfrac12\delta_{\alpha\beta}$
  & 2-D principal-value dipole & conditionally convergent\\
remainder $I - I_\infty$ & --- & numerically, in $k$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\captionof{table}{Split-and-subtract survives, but $\int I_\infty$ is evaluated
in real space.  Only the remainder stays in $k$.}
\end{center}

The traceless part is of degree $-2$ with vanishing angular average --- exactly
the conditional convergence the real-space engine of \S\ref{sec:moments} handles
by peeling derivatives onto the cube faces.  The two representations meet again
here: the same distributional structure, in two dimensions rather than three.

\subsubsection{The principal-value piece vanishes}

In two dimensions $\nabla^2\log\rho = 2\pi\delta^2$, and
$\partial_\alpha\partial_\beta\log\rho = (\delta_{\alpha\beta} -
2\hat{n}_\alpha\hat{n}_\beta)/\rho^2$ away from the origin; taking the trace
fixes the delta weight, so that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pvsplit}
  \partial_\alpha\partial_\beta\log\rho
  = \mathrm{PV}\!\left[\frac{\delta_{\alpha\beta}
      - 2\hat{n}_\alpha\hat{n}_\beta}{\rho^{2}}\right]
    + \pi\,\delta_{\alpha\beta}\,\delta^{2}(\mathbf{x}) .
\end{equation}
By translation invariance the double integral over the square collapses onto the
autocorrelation of its indicator, $w(\mathbf{u}) = (2a-|u_1|)(2a-|u_2|)$, exactly
--- removing two of the four dimensions --- and both derivatives move onto $w$,
which is legitimate because $w$ has compact support.

$w$ is piecewise bilinear, so $\partial_1\partial_1 w$ is a sum of line deltas.
It has three terms, not one: $w$ vanishes at $|u_1| = 2a$ but
$\partial_1 w$ does \emph{not} --- it jumps from $\mp(2a-|u_2|)$ to zero --- so
there are edge deltas at $u_1 = \pm2a$ alongside the interior one at $u_1 = 0$.
With them,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Dab}
  D_{11} = \underbrace{3}_{\text{interior}} + \underbrace{(\pi-3)}_{\text{edges}}
         = \pi = \pi(2a)^2 \Big|_{a=1/2},
  \qquad D_{12} = 0 ,
\end{equation}
and the trace identity $\operatorname{tr}D = \langle\nabla^2\log\rho, w\rangle =
2\pi w(0) = 2\pi(2a)^2$ holds exactly.  Dropping the edge deltas gives $6$ in
place of $2\pi$, so that identity is what detects them.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Hence $\langle \mathrm{PV}_{\alpha\beta}, w\rangle = D_{\alpha\beta} -
\pi\delta_{\alpha\beta}w(0) = 0$: the traceless dipole contributes nothing.}
\end{quote}

It could not have contributed.  $\langle\mathrm{PV}_{\alpha\beta},w\rangle$ is a
symmetric two-tensor invariant under the square's $D_4$ symmetry, and the only
such tensor is a multiple of $\delta_{\alpha\beta}$ --- the traceless part has
nowhere to live.  The conditional convergence is real, but for this cross-section
it integrates away.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{The whole of $\int I_\infty$ is therefore the local delta term.}  The
piece that looked hardest --- the conditionally convergent, shape-dependent
dipole --- is identically zero here, and what remains is elementary.
\end{quote}

\subsubsection{Why the transferred form converges}\label{sec:transferred}

In the untransferred form $\partial_\alpha \to ik_\alpha$, each contrast
operator contributes a factor of $k$, and the pole of \eqref{eq:sincints}
follows.  After the by-parts transfer of \eqref{eq:byparts} the derivatives act
on the \emph{polynomials, in real space}: $\partial_\alpha$ of an affine trial
function is a constant, whose lateral transform is again the box form factor.
No factor of $k$ survives, and the lateral integral converges.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{The augmentation therefore does two things at once:} it keeps the
indicator from being differentiated, and it makes the lateral integral
convergent.  Both are the same integration by parts.
\end{quote}

Convergence is nevertheless slow.  With $\widehat{K}\to C$ the truncation error
falls only as $1/\Lambda$, which is what the subtraction is for.

\subsubsection{The tail, in closed form}

The tail is elementary.  For the in-plane entry
$C = \mu(\hat{k}_1^2-1) = -\mu\,k_2^2/k^2$, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tail}
  \int\! d^2k\,W\,C = -\mu\!\int\! d^2k\,
    \operatorname{sinc}^2(k_1a)\operatorname{sinc}^2(k_2a)\,\frac{k_2^2}{k^2}
  = -\frac{\mu}{2}\Bigl(\frac{\pi}{a}\Bigr)^{\!2},
\end{equation}
because $k_1^2/k^2 + k_2^2/k^2 = 1$ and the weight is symmetric under
$k_1\!\leftrightarrow\!k_2$, so each piece is exactly half of
$\int\!d^2k\,W = (\pi/a)^2$.  No quadrature is required; direct integration
returns $19.7376$ against $(\pi/a)^2/2 = 19.7392$, the difference being the
quadrature's own error on a slowly converging integrand.

\subsection{Step 4: how the augmentation composes with the Schwinger
form}\label{sec:schwinger}

The augmentation of \S\ref{sec:testspace} turns the differential $\Delta A$ into
a multiplicative $\Delta C_{\text{eff}}$ by moving each outer lateral derivative
onto the test function.  That is what keeps $\partial_\alpha \mathbf{1}_V$ ---
the surface layer on the faces of the cube --- from ever being formed.  The
Schwinger object
\begin{equation}\label{eq:schwinger}
  \bigl[\Delta C\,\mathcal{G}\,\Delta C\bigr]_{kl}
  = \bigl\langle J_6\psi_k \bigm| \Delta A\,\Gamma\,\Delta A \bigm| \psi_l\bigr\rangle
\end{equation}
carries \emph{two} contrast operators, and the right-hand one has no test
function next to it: what $\Gamma$ needs is $\Delta A\psi_l$ as an actual
source, which is $\partial_\alpha(\Delta c\,\mathbf{1}_V\,\partial_\beta\psi_l)$
--- precisely the object the transfer exists to avoid.  Read that way the two
constructions look incompatible.

They are not, and the reason is that the Born term and the Schwinger term differ
in a way that matters.  In the Born term the indicator of the contrast and the
test function sit at the \emph{same} point, so $\partial_\alpha(\mathbf{1}_V c)$
would have to be multiplied by another factor supported on $\bar{V}$; a surface
layer times an indicator is not defined, and the transfer is therefore not a
convenience but the only well-posed form.  In the Schwinger term the two
indicators are separated by $\Gamma$.  The source at $\mathbf{x}'$ is paired,
through a kernel, with a test object at $\mathbf{x}$; nothing coincides, the
surface layer is harmless --- and in the lateral Fourier domain it need not be
formed at all, because
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ikrule}
  \mathcal{F}\bigl[\partial_\alpha(\mathbf{1}_V c)\bigr](\mathbf{k})
  = i k_\alpha\, \mathcal{F}\bigl[\mathbf{1}_V c\bigr](\mathbf{k})
\end{equation}
is exact.  The derivative is carried by the \emph{transform}; the indicator
stays inside the form factor, undifferentiated.

The bookkeeping that follows is uniform.  Every derivative in $A$ is lateral ---
the depth derivative is the left-hand side of $\partial_3 q = Aq+d$ --- so in the
lateral Fourier domain each is a factor $ik_\alpha$ at the single wavenumber the
integral runs over.  One entry of \eqref{eq:schwinger} has four derivative slots,
and they are placed as
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
leg & slot & where it goes\\
\midrule
left  & outer & onto the test polynomial \quad(the augmentation)\\
left  & inner & onto the propagated field \quad$\to\ ik_\beta$\\
right & outer & onto the source transform \quad$\to\ ik_\alpha$, by \eqref{eq:ikrule}\\
right & inner & onto the trial polynomial \quad(the augmentation)\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The two outer slots are the ones in question.  The left one goes exactly where
$\Delta C_{\text{eff}}$ already puts it; the right one goes onto the kernel.  No
surface term is evaluated on a face, and the augmentation is unchanged.

\eqref{eq:ikrule} is checked numerically rather than asserted.  Paired with a
smooth bump $f$ it states
$\int f\,\partial_1\mathbf{1}_S = -\int_S \partial_1 f$, whose right side is an
ordinary integral over the square; evaluating the left side in the Fourier
domain as $(2\pi)^{-2}\!\int \hat{f}(-\mathbf{k})\,ik_1 F(\mathbf{k})$ reproduces
it to $1.0\times10^{-13}$, with a zero imaginary residue and the opposite sign
rejected by a factor of two.

\subsubsection*{The propagator, checked against the Kelvin tensor}

Before any of this can be assembled, one normalisation has to be settled that the
bridge identity of \S\ref{sec:tmatrix} does not reach: the bridge compares
$\Gamma$ at two depths and so tests propagation, not the strength of the source
jump.  Inverting $\Gamma$ laterally at a fixed vertical separation and reading
the velocity--force block,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{1}{-i\omega}\,(2\pi)^{-2}\!\!\int\! d^2k\;
  \Gamma_{3+i,\,j}(\mathbf{k};\Delta z)
  \;\;\longrightarrow\;\; G_{ij}(\Delta z),
\end{equation}
the analytic Kelvin tensor comes back to $7.3\times10^{-4}$ and still
converging, with off-diagonal leakage $2.9\times10^{-4}$; the residue is
consistent with the $O((k_S r)^2)$ the static comparison drops.  This fixes both
conventions at once --- that a force $f_i$ enters row $i$ of
$\partial_3 q = Aq + s$, and that $q_{3+i} = v_i = -i\omega u_i$ comes out.  The
quadrature must be polar: the kernel depends on $|\mathbf{k}|$ and on direction
separately and so is not smooth at the origin, where a Cartesian tensor rule
converges only as $1/n$ (measured: $4.0\times10^{-2} \to 1.2\times10^{-2}$ over
a ninefold refinement, against $5.4\times10^{-3} \to 7.3\times10^{-4}$ in
polar).  This is the opposite of the lateral moment itself, whose form factors
are separable sinc products that polar cannot resolve.

\subsubsection*{The density channel, assembled}

With $\Delta\lambda = \Delta\mu = 0$ only the multiplicative $i\omega\Delta\rho$
term of $\Delta A$ survives, both legs reduce to constants, and
\eqref{eq:schwinger} can be predicted in closed form:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:densarb}
  \bigl\langle J_6\psi_0 \bigm| \Delta A\,\Gamma\,\Delta A \bigm| \psi_0\bigr\rangle
  = i\omega^5 \Delta\rho^2\, M,
  \qquad
  M = \int_V\!\!\int_V G_{11} = \Bigl(a_0 + \tfrac{b_0}{3}\Bigr) D_0 ,
\end{equation}
with $a_0, b_0$ the Kelvin coefficients of \S\ref{sec:moments} and $D_0$ the
purely geometric double-volume moment $\int_V\!\int_V 1/r$.  $D_0$ is evaluated
by writing the double integral as a single one against the autocorrelation
weight $\prod_i(2a - |u_i|)$ --- exact, and three dimensions cheaper --- and
removing the $1/r$ outright with a Duffy substitution
$\mathbf{u} = t(\xi,\eta,1)$, whose Jacobian $t^2$ cancels it:
\begin{equation}
  D_0 = 1.8823126444 ,\qquad
  \int_V 1/r = 2.3800773640 .
\end{equation}
The lateral assembly with \emph{both} contrast operators in place reproduces
\eqref{eq:densarb} to $6.9\times10^{-5}$, the two routes sharing nothing but the
medium: one is a spectral construction on $A$ integrated over lateral
wavenumber, the other a real-space moment of the Kelvin tensor.

\subsubsection*{What the nine-parameter $\mathcal{G}$ is, and is not}

The same comparison locates an object that had been taken on trust.  The
$\mathcal{G}$ used in \S\ref{sec:tmatrix} was \emph{calibrated} against this
package's $T_9$ operator rather than derived, and it is not the moment that
\eqref{eq:schwinger} calls for.  The gap factorises exactly:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gapfactor}
  \frac{[\Delta C\,\mathcal{G}\,\Delta C]_{00}}{i\omega^5\Delta\rho^2 M}
  = i\omega\,\frac{\int_V 1/r \;+\; i\,(\text{radiation})}{D_0},
  \qquad \text{residual } 3.6\times10^{-5},
\end{equation}
where the radiation term is the closed form
$V\omega(\alpha^{-3}+2\beta^{-3})/12\pi\rho$ that the moment export is itself
gated on.  Two statements, both of which matter:
\begin{itemize}
\item The geometric factor $\int_V 1/r \big/ D_0 = 1.2644$ is the ratio of a
  \emph{point}-volume moment to a \emph{volume}-volume moment.  The calibrated
  $\mathcal{G}$ is a collocation object; \eqref{eq:schwinger} is a Galerkin one.
  That is consistent with this project's own finding that the single site is
  best posed by collocation, but the two cannot be mixed without the factor.
\item The remaining $i\omega$ is the normalisation of the $q$-field trial space:
  $\Delta C\,\mathcal{G}\,\Delta C$ carries two $J_6$ test factors while
  \eqref{eq:schwinger} carries one, so a $\mathcal{G}$ that composes correctly
  must absorb the trial-function normalisation.  How it should do so is not
  settled here; see \S\ref{sec:open}.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{The derivative channels, and three defects the test exposed}

The density channel settles the basis, the kernel, the $z$ integrals and the
quadrature, but its contrast operator is \emph{multiplicative}: it does not
exercise \eqref{eq:ikrule} at all.  The channels that do are those carrying
$\Delta\lambda$ and $\Delta\mu$, and for them a sharp statement is available with
no new arbiter.  $\Jsix\dA$ is symmetric --- that is what derived the test space
in \S\ref{sec:testspace} --- and $\boldsymbol\Gamma$ is reciprocal,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:recip}
  \boldsymbol\Gamma(-\mathbf{k}; z', z)\tp =
  \Jsix\, \boldsymbol\Gamma(\mathbf{k}; z, z')\, \Jsix ,
\end{equation}
which follows from $\Aop(-\mathbf{k})\tp\Jsix = -\Jsix\Aop(\mathbf{k})$ and
$\Jsix^{-1} = -\Jsix$.  Hence the assembled matrix must be symmetric.  It is not
symmetric by construction: the assembly builds its two legs by opposite rules,
transferring the left outer derivative onto the test polynomial and carrying the
right one on the kernel as $ik$.  Symmetry is therefore a test of exactly the
bookkeeping in question, and on a quadrature grid symmetric under
$\mathbf{k}\to-\mathbf{k}$ it is an \emph{algebraic} property, holding to round-off
even where the grid is far too coarse to give the value accurately.

Applied, it found three defects, each in code that had already passed its own
checks:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The up/down split was decided by round-off inside the propagating
      window.}  Classifying by $\Re(\text{ev}) < 0$ is right for evanescent modes
      and meaningless for propagating ones: below $k = \omega/\beta$ the vertical
      wavenumber is real, $\Re(\text{ev})$ vanishes to arithmetic noise, and its
      sign was being decided independently at $+\mathbf{k}$ and $-\mathbf{k}$.
      The reciprocity residual \eqref{eq:recip} at $|k| = 1.1\times10^{-3}$ was
      $1.245$ --- a complete failure, in the region that carries all of the
      radiation.  The limit $\omega \to \omega(1+i\epsilon)$ settles it without
      needing $\epsilon$: a propagating mode has $\text{ev} = ik_z$ and the
      perturbation gives $k_z$ a positive imaginary part, so $\Re(\text{ev})\to0^-$
      exactly when $\Im(\text{ev}) > 0$.  Residual after: $3.7\times10^{-13}$.
\item \textbf{The triangular $z$ integral cancels catastrophically at small
      $aq$.}  The numerator of $T^{11}$ is $O((aq)^5)$ while its individual terms
      are $O(1)$, so by $aq \approx 0.025$ it has lost about eight digits.  That
      is not a corner case --- it is again the propagating window.  Expanding
      $T^{mn}$ as a power series in $q$ below $|2aq| = 3$, with the coefficients
      of \S\ref{sec:triseries}, took that node from $1.3\times10^{-6}$ to
      $2.1\times10^{-13}$.
\item \textbf{The scaling used to condition the eigenproblem is in the wrong
      direction.}  $\mathrm{diag}(1,1,1,k,k,k)$ was introduced because the up-
      and down-going eigenvectors become parallel as $k$ grows.  Measured against
      \emph{no scaling at all} it makes the eigenvector condition number
      \emph{worse} at every $k$ above the propagating window --- by two orders at
      $k = 60$.  Parlett--Reinsch balancing \cite{parlett1969} beats both by about twelve orders
      there ($8.7\times10^{7}$ against $1.6\times10^{20}$), because it is free to
      treat the third component differently from the first two, which no scalar
      rule can.
\end{enumerate}

With all three repaired the assembled $9\times9$ matrix is symmetric to
$4.7\times10^{-12}$, while reversing the sign of the right leg's $ik$ --- the
one step \eqref{eq:ikrule} licenses --- breaks it at $7.2\times10^{-2}$.  That is
a separation of ten orders on precisely the disputed bookkeeping, and the
derivative terms carry $80\%$ of the matrix, so the test is not vacuous.

\subsubsection*{The radiation reaction, from the first-order system}

Repairing the branch selection changed the physics, not just the arithmetic.  The
previous classification suppressed the outgoing condition; with it restored, the
propagator carries radiation damping, and both real-space arbiters above had to
acquire the term they were missing.  The sharpest form of the check is pointwise:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:radiation}
  \Im G_{11}\bigl(\Delta z\bigr)\Bigm|_{\text{first-order}} = 5.2134\times10^{-14}
  \qquad\text{against}\qquad
  \frac{\omega}{12\pi\rho}\Bigl(\frac{1}{\alpha^3} + \frac{2}{\beta^3}\Bigr)
  = 5.2250\times10^{-14},
\end{equation}
agreeing to $2.2\times10^{-3}$.  This quantity has \emph{no static counterpart at
all}: it is not a correction to the Kelvin tensor but a term the static theory
does not contain, and it arrives here out of the spectral projectors of $\Aop$
with nothing put in by hand.

Resolving it needs the quadrature to respect the two branch radii $|k| =
\omega/\alpha$ and $\omega/\beta$, where the vertical wavenumber vanishes.  Those
are \emph{circles}: no Cartesian panel can follow one, and an unresolved
square-root costs several per cent.  The lateral integral is therefore split ---
Cartesian outside the propagating window, where the integrand is a separable
product of sinc-type form factors that polar cannot resolve; polar inside it,
where the branch radii are panel edges and $ka < 0.02$ makes the form factors
constant to $10^{-4}$.  The central block of the Cartesian grid is exactly the
inner square, so the two tile the box with no overlap and no gap.  With that in
place the density channel converges cleanly --- $9.858$, $9.834$,
$9.828 \times 10^{-4}$ over a fourfold refinement --- where before it drifted.

\emph{Status of Task~1.}  The design question is settled; the assembly computes a

\section{The thesis spectral representation, and what it
removes}\label{sec:thesisspectral}

Three of the four numerical defects of \S\ref{sec:schwinger} are not properties
of the physics.  They are consequences of asking a numerical eigensolver to do
work that \S2.1 of the thesis has already done in closed form, and they do not
arise at all if $\boldsymbol\Gamma$ is built the way the thesis builds it.

\subsection{What the thesis gives}

Alongside (Akdef) the thesis carries the complete spectral
representation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:specA}
  \bA(k_x,k_y) = \mathbf{D}_z\,\boldsymbol\Lambda\,\mathbf{D}_z^{-1},
  \qquad
  \boldsymbol\Lambda = i\,\mathrm{diag}\bigl[k_{z,P},k_{z,S},k_{z,H},
                                            -k_{z,P},-k_{z,S},-k_{z,H}\bigr],
\end{equation}
with the columns of $\mathbf{D}_z$ written out analytically --- $|\pm,P\rangle$,
$|\pm,S\rangle$, $|\pm,H\rangle$, the $+$ columns downgoing --- and three
further things that matter more than they look:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The branch is defined piecewise.}
      $k_{z,c} = \sqrt{\hat K_c^2-k_x^2}$ where $|k_x|<|\hat K_c|$ and
      $i\sqrt{k_x^2-\hat K_c^2}$ otherwise: the positive root in both regimes,
      with the up/down assignment carried by the \emph{column ordering} of
      \eqref{eq:specA} rather than by the sign of any computed quantity.
\item \textbf{The shear basis is chosen non-degenerate.}  The thesis notes
      $k_{z,S} = k_{z,H}$ and keeps the two labelled apart regardless, remarking
      that its quasi-$SV$ and quasi-$SH$ vectors --- unlike the conventional
      seismological decomposition --- remain independent at $k_x=k_y=0$.
\item \textbf{The inverse requires no inversion.}  From the quasi-Hamiltonian
      relation,
      \begin{equation}\label{eq:dzinv}
        \mathbf{D}_z^{-1}(k_x,k_y)
          = -i\,\Jsix\,\mathbf{D}_z\tp(-k_x,-k_y)\,\Jsix .
      \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

All of it is verified here rather than taken on trust, and the checks are
coupled: \eqref{eq:specA} cannot reproduce (Akdef) unless the
eigenvectors, the ordering, the branch and \eqref{eq:dzinv} are all correct
together.  It does, to $6.2\times10^{-14}$.  The normalisation factors
$\epsilon_c$, which the thesis specifies only as ``chosen so that
$\mathbf{D}_z^{-1}$ takes a simple form'', are \emph{read off} rather than
guessed: with $\epsilon=1$ the product \eqref{eq:dzinv}$\,\cdot\mathbf{D}_z$ is
diagonal --- to $1.1\times10^{-15}$, itself a test of the eigenvectors --- and
$\epsilon_c^2$ is the reciprocal of each diagonal entry.

\paragraph{The one place $\mathbf{D}_z$ is genuinely singular, and why it is not
a difficulty.}  Reading $\epsilon_c$ off the diagonal fails where a diagonal
entry vanishes, and that is not round-off: it is the branch point $k_{z,c} = 0$,
at $|k| = \hat K_P$ and $|k| = \hat K_S$ exactly, where that wave type's up- and
down-going columns coalesce and $\mathbf{D}_z$ has rank five.  It must be tested
on $k_{z,c}$ itself and not on the spread of the diagonal --- the entries of
$\mathbf{D}_z$ span many orders at moderate $k$, so a smallest-entry threshold
measures dynamic range and fires where nothing is wrong.

The degeneracy exists only in the lossless idealisation.  Since
$k_{z,c}^2 = \hat K_c^2 - k^2$ and $\hat K_c = \omega/c$, it cannot vanish at
real $k$ once $\omega$ carries an imaginary part, so the attenuation that
\S\ref{sec:propmom} already adopts for the quadrature --- ``$\omega$ is complex
from the outset'' --- removes the coalescence outright rather than dodging it.
At $Q = 200$ the closest approach of $|k_{z,S}|$ across its own circle is
$1.4\times10^{-3}$ against a scale of $2\times10^{-2}$; $\mathbf{D}_z$ inverts
\emph{on} the circle to $1.2\times10^{-15}$, where the lossless evaluation is
singular; and \eqref{eq:specA} is rebuilt there to $3.7\times10^{-16}$.  The
device is the one the package already uses: putting the loss in a complex
slowness rather than a complex $\omega$ is the same statement, since $k_{z,c}$
depends on the two only through $\omega/c$, and the two agree to
$6.3\times10^{-6}$ at $Q = 200$ --- the $O(Q^{-2})$ difference.  Nudging a node
off the point remains the fallback for a deliberately lossless calculation, and
placing a panel edge there, as the lateral gates do, is the right treatment of
the integrable $1/\sqrt{\cdot}$ that survives in that limit.

\section{A layer in a whole space: the external
arbiter}\label{sec:layer}

Every comparison so far in this note sets two formulations against each other.
None of them says which is right.  A single layer embedded in a whole space
removes that limitation, because its reflection coefficient is known
independently --- from a validated reflectivity recursion \cite{kennett1983}, and from a closed
form short enough to derive here --- and because it exercises the operator in a
way the cube cannot.

\subsection{Why a layer is the right test}

For a laterally invariant contrast the operator difference is simply
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dalayer}
  \dA(z) = \bigl[\Aop_{\text{total}}(\mathbf{k}) -
                 \Aop_{\text{background}}(\mathbf{k})\bigr]\,
           \mathbf{1}_{[0,h]}(z),
\end{equation}
a piecewise-constant matrix.  \textbf{There is no augmentation, no by-parts
transfer, no surface term and no differentiated indicator}, because $\Aop$
carries no $\partial_3$: a jump in the medium is simply a jump in the
coefficients of an ODE, and is harmless.  That is the structural advantage of
the first-order form, and it is precisely the property the whole cube
development of \S\ref{sec:testspace}--\S\ref{sec:schwinger} had to work around.
So this tests $\Aop$, $\boldsymbol\Gamma$ and the Lippmann--Schwinger
composition with none of the cube machinery in the way.

It also tests two things the cube checks could not.  It is \emph{nonlinear} in
the contrast, so it reaches the resummation rather than the Born term alone; and
$\dA$ carries $\mathbf{k}$ explicitly through $\Aop_{12} \sim \nu_1k^2/i\omega$,
so scanning slowness tests a $k$ dependence that the density channel of
\S\ref{sec:schwinger}, being multiplicative, left untouched.

\subsection{The closed form, by continuity}

At normal incidence the answer follows from matching.  With $e^{-i\omega t}$ and
$u$ the vertical displacement, each medium carries
$u = D e^{ikz} + U e^{-ikz}$ with $k = \omega/c$, and the traction is
$\tau = \rho c^2 \partial_z u = i\rho c\,\omega\,(D e^{ikz} - U e^{-ikz})$.
Continuity of $u$ and $\tau$ at $z=0$ and $z=h$, with no up-going wave below,
gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normallayer}
  R = \frac{r_{12} + r_{23}\,e^{2i\omega h/\alpha_2}}
           {1 + r_{12}r_{23}\,e^{2i\omega h/\alpha_2}},
  \qquad
  r_{12} = \frac{Z_1-Z_2}{Z_1+Z_2} = -r_{23},
  \qquad Z = \rho c .
\end{equation}
The point of deriving it rather than quoting it is that the \emph{sign} is then
fixed by the derivation.  Throughout this section
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rdef}
  R \;=\;
  \frac{u_3 \text{ of the up-going field at } z=0^-}
       {u_3 \text{ of the incident field at } z=0^-},
\end{equation}
on both sides of every comparison, which leaves no convention free.

\subsection{The first-order route}

The laterally invariant Lippmann--Schwinger equation
\begin{equation}
  q(z) = q_{\text{inc}}(z) +
         \int_0^h \boldsymbol\Gamma(\mathbf{k}; z,z')\,\dA\, q(z')\,dz'
\end{equation}
is solved by piecewise-constant collocation, the cell integrals taken in closed
form from the spectral representation of $\boldsymbol\Gamma$ and \emph{split at
$z'=z$}: the jump across the diagonal is the source delta, and quadrature across
it would smear exactly the object that defines the propagator.

One structural simplification is used, and it is exact.  With $\mathbf{k}$ along
$x_1$ the system is block diagonal: $\{-\tau_{23}, v_2\}$ is $SH$ and does not
couple to $\{-\tau_{13}, -\tau_{33}, v_1, v_3\}$.  Working in the $4\times4$
$P$-$SV$ block is therefore no approximation --- and it removes the double
degeneracy of $q_S$, which is a genuine hazard rather than a nicety: a
degenerate eigenspace has no preferred basis, so \texttt{eig} returns an
arbitrary mixture of $SV$ and $SH$.  Before the block restriction the $SS$
coefficient was correct at three slownesses and wrong \emph{by a factor of four}
at the fourth, which reads as a critical angle and is linear algebra.

$1.00008$, $1.00010$, $1.00008$.

Finally, a control isolates what this test uniquely reaches.  Building $\dA$ at
$\mathbf{k}=0$ while leaving $\boldsymbol\Gamma$ at the true $\mathbf{k}$ changes
\emph{nothing} at normal incidence (relative difference $0$, exactly) and breaks
the answer by $6.8\times10^{-2}$ off it.  The $k$ dependence of the contrast
operator is therefore load-bearing, and correct.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{What this establishes, and what it does not.}  The system matrix $\Aop$,
the propagator $\boldsymbol\Gamma$, their composition, and the resummation
reproduce an independently validated reflectivity code across contrast and
across angle.  That is the first external validation in this note.  It does
\emph{not} validate the cube: the augmentation, the form factors and the moments
of \S\ref{sec:moments} play no part in a laterally invariant problem.  Those
remain checked only against other formulations.
\end{quote}

\section{The static moments \texorpdfstring{$A$, $B$ and $C$}{A, B and C},
read off}\label{sec:bmoment}

This is what Task~1 was for: the cube's propagator moment obtained from
$\partial_3\bq = \Aop\bq + \bd$ rather than from the Kelvin tensor, and compared
with a closed form the first-order machinery knows nothing about.

\subsection{Which realisation, and why it is the easy one}

$B$ multiplies $(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jm} + \delta_{im}\delta_{jk})$ in the moment
tensor, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:brealise}
  B = I_{xyxy} = \int_V \partial_y\partial_x G_{xy}\,dV ,
\end{equation}
and \emph{that} realisation carries only lateral derivatives.  In the lateral
Fourier domain they are factors of $ik$, with no $\partial_z$ and hence no
$\Aop\boldsymbol\Gamma$ product to form:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bintegral}
  B = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^2}\int\! d^2k\;(ik_x)(ik_y)\,F(\mathbf{k})
      \int_{-a}^{a}\! G_{xy}(\mathbf{k}; -z')\,dz' .
\end{equation}
Every other realisation of $B$ involves $\partial_z$.  The $z$ integral is closed
form from \eqref{eq:specA}; the lateral one is quadrature.

\subsection{The sign, pinned independently}

The two factors of $i$ give $(ik_x)(ik_y) = -k_xk_y$.  Dropping them costs
exactly a sign, which is invisible against $|B|$ and would be reported as
success.  So the sign is fixed by an evaluation owing nothing to the first-order
machinery: by the divergence theorem,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bsurface}
  \int_V \partial_y\partial_x G_{xy}\,dV
  = \oint_{\partial V} n_y\,\bigl(\partial_x G_{xy}\bigr)\,dA ,
\end{equation}
which is legitimate --- and, more to the point, \emph{complete} --- because the
divergence theorem applied to a locally integrable $F$ returns the full
\emph{distributional} integral.  Any delta at the origin is therefore included
automatically, which is what makes this a safe way to evaluate a quantity whose
volume form has an Eshelby delta in it.  Here $\partial_x G_{xy} \sim 1/r^2$,
integrable in three dimensions and non-singular on faces standing at distance $a$
from the centre; only the $y=\pm a$ faces contribute and their integrands add,
being odd in $y$.  Evaluated,
\begin{equation}
  \oint_{\partial V} n_y \partial_x G_{xy}\,dA = +2.6137073561\times10^{-12}
  = B \quad\text{to } 1.7\times10^{-13},
\end{equation}
so \eqref{eq:brealise} is the right realisation and the sign is positive.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{$A$ and $C$}{A and C}, and the delta they
carry}\label{sec:acmoment}

The same index map gives the other two with lateral derivatives alone:
\begin{equation}
  I_{xxyy} = A, \qquad I_{xxxx} = A + 2B + C,
\end{equation}
so nothing here needs $\partial_z$ either.  What they do have, and $B$ does not,
is the Eshelby delta.  It lives in the $\delta_{ij}$ channels; $B$ has $i\neq j$
and so carries none, which is why its integral converges outright while these
two do not.  \emph{Convergence tracking the delta's presence is a check on the
setup in itself.}

\subsubsection*{The split closes by representation, not by magnitude}

Since $\widehat{g}\to C(\theta)/k^2$, the asymptotic integrand is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:asymint}
  -k_x^2 F(\mathbf{k})\,\frac{C(\theta)}{k^2}
  = -\cos^2\!\theta\; C(\theta)\, F(\mathbf{k}),
\end{equation}
a function of \emph{direction} only.  Its $k$-space integral is a pure pole with
no finite part, so adding it back restores exactly the divergence the
subtraction was introduced to remove: \textbf{the split cannot be closed in
$k$-space at all}, as \S\ref{sec:propmom} already found for the tail there.  It
closes by representation instead.

\emph{The remainder stays in $k$}, and is regular at the origin --- which is the
point, because $-k_x^2$ times $-C/k^2$ is $+\cos^2\!\theta\,C$, bounded.  No
taper, no cutoff, no matching of one region to another: it is independent of the
box half-width to seven digits across a fourfold change, where the unsubtracted
integral drifted by per cent.

\emph{The local part goes to real space.}  Writing
$h(\theta) = -\cos^2\!\theta\,C(\theta) = \sum_n h_n e^{in\theta}$, the $n=0$
term is a delta contributing $h_0\,\mathbf{1}_S(0) = h_0$.  For $n\neq0$ the
inverse transform of $e^{in\theta}$ is a homogeneous distribution of degree
$-2$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pvdipole}
  \check{f}_n(r,\varphi) = \frac{(-i)^n |n|}{2\pi}\,\frac{e^{in\varphi}}{r^{2}},
  \qquad \int_0^\infty\! k\,J_n(kr)\,dk = \frac{|n|}{r^{2}},
\end{equation}
whose principal-value integral over the square is elementary, the $\ln\epsilon$
of $\int_\epsilon^R dr/r$ cancelling because $e^{in\varphi}$ has zero angular
mean:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pvsquare}
  \int_S \frac{e^{in\varphi}}{r^{2}}\,d^2x
  = \int_0^{2\pi}\! e^{in\varphi}\,\ln R(\varphi)\,d\varphi,
  \qquad R(\varphi) = \frac{a}{\max(|\cos\varphi|,|\sin\varphi|)} .
\end{equation}
$D_4$ symmetry admits $n = 0, \pm4, \pm8,\dots$, and the measurement says the
series stops at the first of them: $n=4$ carries the whole correction and
everything above it contributes $4\times10^{-9}$ relative.  Taking the local
term as the delta alone is wrong by $5.5\%$, so the dipole is load-bearing and
not decorative --- both are gated.

\subsection{Two things that nearly went wrong}

\emph{The grid.}  The integrand is $4\sin(k_xa)\sin(k_ya)$ times the kernel, a
product of sincs separable in the \emph{Cartesian} components.  On a polar grid
it does not converge even at fixed cutoff, and that drift, read as cutoff
dependence, looks exactly like a logarithmic divergence --- which invites a hunt
for a missing Eshelby delta.  There is none.  On Cartesian panels cut at the sinc
half-period $\pi/a$ the integral converges cleanly.  The resolution study at
\emph{fixed} cutoff is what separates the two readings, and nothing else does.

$2.6147\times10^{-12}$ --- agreeing with $B$ to $3.8\times10^{-4}$, and entirely
an artefact of where the truncation happened to land.

column $i$ of $\mathbf{D}_z$ and divides row $i$ of its inverse, so it cancels out
of every projector.  Dropping it removes the one step in the construction that
cancels --- the step of \S\ref{sec:thesisspectral} that limits the useful range in
$k$ --- and lets the whole assembly batch.

\section{Which formulation is more accurate}\label{sec:whichbetter}

\S\ref{sec:layer} validates the operator and the propagator but cannot compare
the two formulations, because a laterally invariant problem is one the cube
moment method does not address at all.  The geometry that does compare them is
the one this package already uses to score itself: a periodic patch of
space-filling cubes, which for identical cubes \emph{is} a homogeneous layer, and
whose reflection coefficient the reflectivity recursion gives exactly.  Both
routes then build a single-site $T$ for the same cube, both pass through the same
lattice sum, the same contact correction and the same Foldy--Lax \cite{foldy1945,lax1951} solve, and both
are scored against the same reference.  Only $T_0$ differs.

\subsection{Substituting at the one place both routes speak the same language}

$\mathbf{T} = \dC(\mathbf{I}-\mathcal{G}\dC)^{-1}$ is not in the package's
$9\times9$ convention --- the strain block differs in sign and by the engineering
doubling, and $\dCeff$ is in velocity units besides (\S\ref{sec:units}).  Mapping
all of that would risk measuring a convention as though it were physics.  The
substitution is therefore made through the four \emph{dimensionless}
amplification factors $A_u$, $A_\theta$, $A_{e,\mathrm{diag}}$,
$A_{e,\mathrm{off}}$, which are ratios and carry no units.  The package's own
mapping to renormalised contrasts and its own $T_0$ construction are then used
unchanged for both routes, so nothing distinguishes them but those four numbers.
What licenses this is that the Navier route reproduces the package's own factors
to $1.5\times10^{-6}$, re-checked rather than assumed.

\subsection{Why this is not cancellation}

At weak contrast the two $T_0$ differ by $1.8\times10^{-5}$ and yet both miss the
reference by $2.1\times10^{-3}$: \textbf{most of the error is shared}, and belongs
to the lattice rather than to $T_0$.  A difference in a mostly-shared error is
exactly the shape an accidental cancellation would take --- the first-order
$O(\Delta c^2)$ term happening to offset the lattice's own.  Refining the lattice
discriminates between the two readings:

The shared error falls and the advantage \emph{grows}.  Cancellation would erode

attributable to $T_0$, not an estimate of it --- it is still improving at the
finest lattice tested.

A remark on how that check was posed, since the first version of it was wrong:
it demanded that the ratio be \emph{stable} under refinement, and it failed.
Stability is not what should be expected.  As the shared error goes to zero the
ratio should approach the $T_0$-attributable value, and monotone movement towards
a limit --- not constancy --- is the correct signature.

\section{The layered background}\label{sec:layered}

This is the payoff, and it is also where the route stops having a competitor.
Everything up to here has had a closed-form whole-space $\mathbf{G}$ standing
behind it, so the moment method could be scored against something.  Over a stack
there is no such object, and $\boldsymbol\Gamma$ must simply \emph{be} the
stratified propagator.

\subsection{Nothing here is new machinery}

The stratified $6\times6$ already exists and is validated, and
\S\ref{sec:bridge} has already measured that it is the Green's matrix of
\emph{this} $\bA$.  What it is not is a function of depth: the block-Riccati
sweep is indexed by \emph{interface}, while the moment integral needs
$\boldsymbol\Gamma(z,z')$ at continuously varying $z$ and $z'$.

The tempting repair --- propagate away from the nearest interface with
$\exp(\bA\,\delta)$ --- is the Thomson--Haskell instability reintroduced
deliberately, and an earlier attempt did exactly that: a propagator-matrix
traversal that reduced correctly in the no-contrast limit, to
$7.4\times10^{-15}$, and was unusable, $\mathrm{cond}(\mathbf{Y}_{\text{up}})$
reaching $9.0\times10^{15}$, because forming $\exp(\bA\,\delta)$ forms the
growing evanescent branch explicitly.  It was discarded rather than patched.

The stable construction does not propagate at all.  The model is \emph{cut} at
the depths wanted, with zero contrast across the cut; the sweep is unchanged, is
still a Riccati recursion, and still never forms the growing branch.  Cutting
must then be harmless, and is: between two \emph{fixed} physical depths, two
cuts and six cuts agree to $2.5\times10^{-15}$ across the propagating and
evanescent regimes alike.  The bridge identity, previously verified only at the
interfaces of a fixed stack, is then re-measured at depths lying on no lattice
and holds to a fraction of one arithmetic floor.

\subsection{The source normalisation, which the bridge could not see}

\S\ref{sec:bridge} tests a \emph{ratio} of two receiver depths.  The source-side
operator cancels out of a ratio, so that gate says nothing about how
$\boldsymbol\Gamma$ is normalised at the source --- and the normalisation is
what makes it a Green's matrix rather than merely a solution.  Measuring it
needs both receivers carried back onto the source plane,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:jumpmeasured}
  e^{-\bA\delta}\,\boldsymbol\Gamma(z'{+}\delta,z')
  \;-\;
  e^{+\bA\delta}\,\boldsymbol\Gamma(z'{-}\delta,z')
  \;=\; \Isix ,
\end{equation}
and \eqref{eq:jumpmeasured} holds to $7\times10^{-14}$, flat in $\delta$ over a
factor of eight.  So the defining relation \eqref{eq:gammadef} holds
with \emph{no} calibration constant anywhere, and the $6\times6$ half of the
source-convention question is closed.  Reciprocity in the thesis basis,
$\boldsymbol\Gamma(-\mathbf{k};z',z)\tp =
\Jsix\boldsymbol\Gamma(\mathbf{k};z,z')\Jsix$ with source and receiver
exchanged, holds to $1.2\times10^{-15}$.

\subsection{The uniform limit, which cannot be what was planned}

The plan called for the uniform limit of the layered assembly to reduce to the
whole space.  It cannot, and the reason is structural rather than numerical:
layer $0$ of the stratified solver is acoustic, so a uniform \emph{elastic}
whole space is not a model the solver can be given.  The ocean floor is always
there and always reflects.

What replaces it is sharper, and has no free parameter.  Whatever the ocean
floor returns is, at \emph{every} receiver, downgoing --- it left the source
upward, turned once, and is coming back down --- and the half-space below
radiates, so nothing else can contribute.  Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uniformlimit}
  \boldsymbol\Gamma_{\text{layered}}(z,z') -
  \boldsymbol\Gamma_{\text{whole space}}(z-z')
  \;=\; \bP_{d}\,\bigl[\,\cdot\,\bigr],
  \qquad
  \bP_{d} = \mathbf{D}_z\,\mathrm{diag}(1,1,1,0,0,0)\,\mathbf{D}_z^{-1},
\end{equation}
with the split taken from the column ordering of \eqref{eq:specA} and nothing
classified numerically.  Measured, $\|\bP_u\Delta\|/\|\Delta\|$ is
$2.6\times10^{-16}$ to $5.2\times10^{-14}$ over five wavenumbers and three
receiver depths, \emph{including} receivers above the source, where the
whole-space term is upgoing and the difference is still not.  The difference
itself is $54\%$--$74\%$ of $\|\boldsymbol\Gamma\|$, so the projection is not
being satisfied by a vanishing numerator.

Beyond the critical wavenumber the returned field is evanescent over the whole
round trip and the difference vanishes outright: at $k_x = 6,7,9$ against
$\omega/\beta = 5.66$, $\|\Delta\|/\|\boldsymbol\Gamma\|$ is
$3\times10^{-15}$--$3\times10^{-14}$.  There the layered $\boldsymbol\Gamma$
\emph{is} the whole-space $\boldsymbol\Gamma$, to the arithmetic floor --- which
is the reduction the plan wanted, holding exactly where the boundary cannot
reach and failing elsewhere only by the boundary term it should fail by.

The control matters here, because \eqref{eq:uniformlimit} is a statement about a
projection and a projection can be satisfied for uninteresting reasons.  Moving
the contrast \emph{below} the receiver instead sends an upgoing field back, and
$\|\bP_u\Delta\|/\|\Delta\|$ rises from $10^{-16}$ to
$1.9\times10^{-1}$--$4.8\times10^{-1}$: twelve orders, on the same geometry.

\section{From the integral equation to invariant
imbedding}\label{sec:imbedding}

\begin{quote}
\small\itshape
Status.  The equivalence of \S\ref{sec:leibniz} is a derivation, and its pivot
--- that the jump is exactly $\Isix$ --- is measured, at $7\times10^{-14}$.
Everything from \eqref{eq:riccatiR} to \S\ref{sec:impmarch} is built and gated:
the coefficient matrices and their conditioning, the impedance form and the
wavenumber range it reaches, the march itself, and its score against a closed
form and against Kennett, on a graded slab where no closed form exists.  These
report measurements, not expectations.

The laterally coupled case of \S\ref{sec:imbedlateral} --- where $\dA$ varies
laterally and couples $\mathbf{k}_\perp$, so that $\bY$ is a matrix on
$(\text{3 components})\times(\text{lateral grid})$ --- is built and gated in
$2\tfrac12$-D, the thesis's own geometry.  \emph{What is not built} is the
extension to full three-dimensional lateral structure, and the end-to-end
comparison against the thesis's architecture \eqref{eq:cbpdef} on a problem
both could solve; \S\ref{sec:imbedlateral} says what that leaves open.
\end{quote}

\subsection{The kernel is one-sided, so the integral equation splits}
\label{sec:leibniz}

The propagator of \S\ref{sec:thesisspectral} is one-sided by construction:
downgoing projectors below the source, upgoing above,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:onesided}
  \boldsymbol\Gamma(z-z') =
  \begin{cases}
    +\sum_{i\,\in\,\text{down}} e^{\lambda_i (z-z')}\,\bP_i, & z>z',\\[2pt]
    -\sum_{i\,\in\,\text{up}} e^{\lambda_i (z-z')}\,\bP_i,   & z<z',
  \end{cases}
  \qquad \bP_d + \bP_u = \Isix .
\end{equation}
The Lippmann--Schwinger equation therefore splits at the \emph{receiver} --- down
from above, up from below:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lssplit}
  \bq(z) = \bq^{0}(z)
  + \int_{-\infty}^{z}\!\boldsymbol\Gamma_{d}(z-z')\,\dA(z')\,\bq(z')\,dz'
  + \int_{z}^{\infty}\!\boldsymbol\Gamma_{u}(z-z')\,\dA(z')\,\bq(z')\,dz' .
\end{equation}

Both limits of integration move with $z$, so differentiating
\eqref{eq:lssplit} produces two Leibniz boundary terms in addition to the
interior derivative:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:leibniz}
  \partial_z\bq =
  \underbrace{\bA\bq^{0}}_{\partial_z\bq^{0}}
  + \underbrace{\bA\,(\bq-\bq^{0})}_{\partial_z\boldsymbol\Gamma\,=\,\bA\boldsymbol\Gamma}
  + \underbrace{\boldsymbol\Gamma_{d}(0^{+})\,\dA\,\bq}_{\text{upper limit}}
  - \underbrace{\boldsymbol\Gamma_{u}(0^{-})\,\dA\,\bq}_{\text{lower limit}} .
\end{equation}
By \eqref{eq:onesided} the two boundary terms are $\bP_d\,\dA\,\bq$ and
$+\bP_u\,\dA\,\bq$, and they sum to $(\bP_d+\bP_u)\dA\,\bq = \Isix\,\dA\,\bq$.
Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lsisode}
  \boxed{\;\partial_z\bq = \bigl[\bA + \dA(z)\bigr]\bq\;}
\end{equation}
--- the integral equation and the local first-order system with the \emph{total}
operator are the same statement.

Two things are worth naming about \eqref{eq:lsisode}.

\paragraph{The jump is load-bearing, not bookkeeping.}  The coefficient of
$\dA$ in \eqref{eq:lsisode} is exactly one \emph{because} $\bP_d+\bP_u = \Isix$.
Any other normalisation of $\boldsymbol\Gamma$ would leave a constant standing
there and the equivalence would be false.  That is the quantity
\eqref{eq:jumpmeasured} measures on the stratified object, at
$7\times10^{-14}$ and flat in $\delta$; \S\ref{sec:layered} presents it as
closing a convention question, and this is the reason it was worth closing.

\paragraph{What the integral form carries that the ODE does not.}  The ODE needs
boundary conditions; \eqref{eq:lssplit} already has them, in the one-sidedness of
its kernel --- radiation at $\pm\infty$ is built into which projectors appear on
which side.  The integral equation is the ODE \emph{plus} its boundary
conditions, which is exactly why it is the useful form to discretise.

\subsection{One-sidedness makes the discretised operator tridiagonal}

Because the kernel is one-sided, a depth discretisation of \eqref{eq:lssplit}
couples only neighbouring depths.  The thesis states this directly: in
Chapter~5 the array $(\bI - \mathcal{S}_{int}\mathcal{E})$ --- the discretised
Lippmann--Schwinger operator --- is \emph{block tridiagonal}
(\texttt{GstratRep.tex}, eq.~\texttt{Amat}), and block-tridiagonal elimination
\emph{is} the Riccati recursion, given there as Algorithm~5.1.  The
Introduction names the connection: Riccati factorisation for discretely layered
media is ``implicit in the invariant embedding schemes of Kennett~(1984),
Tromp \& Snieder~(1988)'', after Chin, Hedstrom \& Thigpen~(1984) and Ha~(1985).

So the three descriptions --- the split integral equation, the two-point
boundary-value ODE, and the invariant imbedding --- are one object seen three
ways, and the sibling repository's sweep is its discretisation.

\subsection{The imbedding equation}

Write \eqref{eq:lsisode} in mode coordinates, $\bq = \mathbf{D}_z\mathbf{a}$ with
$\mathbf{a} = (\mathbf{a}_d,\mathbf{a}_u)$ split by the column ordering of
\eqref{eq:specA}, and let
$\bV = \mathbf{D}_z^{-1}\,\dA\,\mathbf{D}_z$ carry the same block structure:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:modeode}
  \mathbf{a}_d' = \Lambda_d\mathbf{a}_d + \bV_{dd}\mathbf{a}_d + \bV_{du}\mathbf{a}_u,
  \qquad
  \mathbf{a}_u' = \Lambda_u\mathbf{a}_u + \bV_{ud}\mathbf{a}_d + \bV_{uu}\mathbf{a}_u .
\end{equation}
Define $\bR(z)$ as the operator returning the upgoing amplitude produced by
everything \emph{below} $z$ in response to the downgoing amplitude there,
$\mathbf{a}_u(z) = \bR(z)\,\mathbf{a}_d(z)$.  Substituting into
\eqref{eq:modeode} and requiring the result to hold for every $\mathbf{a}_d$
gives the matrix Riccati equation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:riccatiR}
  \bR' = \bV_{ud}
        + \Lambda_u\bR - \bR\,\Lambda_d
        + \bV_{uu}\bR - \bR\,\bV_{dd}
        - \bR\,\bV_{du}\,\bR ,
  \qquad \bR = \mathbf{0} \ \text{below the heterogeneity}.
\end{equation}
The initial condition is the radiation condition, and it is literally the
sibling repository's \texttt{Y[M+1] = 0}: nothing comes back from the radiating
half-space, so the sweep starts from zero reflection and marches up.

\paragraph{Why this is the stable form.}  $\bR$ is a reflection operator and is
bounded --- in an energy norm, $\|\bR\|\le1$ for a passive medium --- so
integrating \eqref{eq:riccatiR} never forms a growing exponential.  A propagator
matrix does form one, which is the Thomson--Haskell instability and is what the
discarded build of \S\ref{sec:layered} ran into at
$\mathrm{cond} = 9.0\times10^{15}$.  The nonlinearity $-\bR\bV_{du}\bR$ is not a
complication to be removed: it is the resummation of the two-way multiple
scattering, and it is what keeps the marched quantity bounded.

\subsection{What this replaces, and what it does not}

For a laterally invariant contrast, \eqref{eq:riccatiR} supersedes the moment
assembly this note has been building toward.  There is no moment integral, no
split-and-subtract, and --- the substantive point --- \textbf{no large-wavenumber
treatment at all}.  The delta has been absorbed \emph{analytically}, by the
Leibniz boundary terms of \eqref{eq:leibniz}, rather than subtracted
numerically.  Everything in \S\ref{sec:acmoment} exists to handle a singularity
that, in this formulation, never has to be represented.
$\boldsymbol\Gamma$ stops being an object to build and becomes an object to
factorise.

That is a statement about the \emph{background}, and it is worth being exact
about how far it reaches.  It does not retire the single-site $T$: a compact
scatterer in a Foldy--Lax solve needs a $T_0$, and a $T_0$ is not what a depth
march produces.  The two decompositions answer different questions, and the
moment route remains the one that answers the second.

\subsection{Building the coefficient matrices}\label{sec:vblocks}

Every piece of $\bV = \mathbf{D}_z^{-1}\,\dA\,\mathbf{D}_z$ already exists.
$\dA$ is the difference of two (Akdef) matrices; $\mathbf{D}_z$ is analytic,
from (Peigen), (SVeigen) and (SHeigen); and $\mathbf{D}_z^{-1}$ comes from
\eqref{eq:dzinv}, with the balanced numerical inverse taking over where
\S\ref{sec:thesisspectral} says it must.  The row scaling cancels out of $\bV$
identically --- $(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{D})^{-1}(\mathbf{R}\dA\mathbf{R}^{-1})
(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{D}) = \mathbf{D}^{-1}\dA\mathbf{D}$ --- so the two routes must
agree, and they do, to $2\times10^{-16}$, which checks both.

$\dA$ is sparse for a structural reason: every entry of (Akdef) built from
$\mathbf{k}$ alone is kinematic and cancels in the difference, leaving $12$ of
$36$.  The compliance block is exactly diagonal,
$\mathrm{diag}(\Delta a,\Delta b,\Delta b)$, so $\bV_{du}$ has a three-term
source; the inertia block carries $\Delta\rho\,\omega^2$ and the
$\Delta\zeta,\Delta\chi,\Delta\mu$ pair; and the two remaining blocks are
nilpotent and are each other's negative transpose.

That last is not a coincidence.  $\dA$ inherits (ATdef) from the two operators it
differences, and \eqref{eq:dzinv} carries the relation into the mode basis:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vham}
  \Jsix\,\bV\tp(-\mathbf{k}) + \bV(\mathbf{k})\,\Jsix = 0
  \iff
  \begin{cases}
    \bV_{du}(\mathbf{k}) = \bV_{du}\tp(-\mathbf{k}),\\
    \bV_{ud}(\mathbf{k}) = \bV_{ud}\tp(-\mathbf{k}),\\
    \bV_{dd}(\mathbf{k}) = -\bV_{uu}\tp(-\mathbf{k}),
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
verified to $3\times10^{-14}$.  Only half the blocks are independent, and
\eqref{eq:riccatiR} preserves the symmetry of $\bR$ --- which is reciprocity of
the reflection operator, and a gate rather than an assumption.

\subsection{Is the march stiff?}\label{sec:stiff}

Yes, and the answer is more useful than that, because of \emph{where} the
stiffness sits.  The linear part of \eqref{eq:riccatiR} is diagonal on the
entries of $\bR$: entry $(c,c')$ sees
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stiffrate}
  \mu_{cc'} = -\bigl(\lambda_{u,c} - \lambda_{d,c'}\bigr)
            = -i\,\bigl(k_{z,c} + k_{z,c'}\bigr).
\end{equation}
Inside the propagating window $k_z$ is real, so \eqref{eq:stiffrate} is purely
imaginary: the march is \emph{oscillatory}, not stiff, and the step is set by
resolving the phase.  Beyond it $k_z = i|k_z|$ and $\mu_{cc'}$ is real and
negative marching upward --- decaying, at a rate up to $2|\mathbf{k}|$.  That is
stiffness, and the ratio grows as $|\mathbf{k}|\beta/\omega$: measured
$1.0,\,1.3,\,3.5,\,10.6,\,35.5,\,106.4$ at
$|\mathbf{k}| = 0.5,\,8,\,20,\,60,\,200,\,600$ against
$\omega/\beta = 5.66$.

But \eqref{eq:stiffrate} is \emph{diagonal}, so an integrating factor removes it
exactly and for nothing: march
$\tilde{\bR} = e^{-\Lambda_u z}\,\bR\,e^{\Lambda_d z}$ and no stiff linear term
remains.  No implicit solver is needed, and a general-purpose stiff integrator
would be the wrong tool.

This is not a new prescription.  It is what the sibling repository's layer
recursion already does: in a homogeneous layer $\bV = 0$ and the exact update is
$\bR \leftarrow e^{\Lambda_u h}\,\bR\,e^{-\Lambda_d h}$, which is precisely the
phase factor it applies.  The recursion is an exponential integrator with the
layer as its step, which is why it is stable at any layer thickness.

\subsection{The real obstruction is non-normality}\label{sec:nonnormal}

Stiffness turns out not to be the difficulty.  Physics requires the spectrum of
$\bV$ to scale with $\Lambda\sim|\mathbf{k}|$ --- a fixed material contrast
cannot strengthen with lateral wavenumber --- and it does:
$\max|\mathrm{eig}\,\bV|/2|\mathbf{k}|$ is flat at $0.0656$ over four decades,
$|\mathbf{k}| = 2$ to $2000$.  The \emph{norm} does not.
$\|\bV\|/2|\mathbf{k}|$ runs $0.07,\,1.8,\,16.7,\,186,\,1671,\,18566$ over the
same range, growing as $|\mathbf{k}|^2$.  Nor is it a scaling choice:
Parlett--Reinsch balancing of $\bV$ returns the identity, so the non-normality
is structural --- \eqref{eq:vham} pins $\bV_{du}$ and $\bV_{ud}$ to comparable
size and leaves a diagonal rescaling nothing to do.

The mechanism is measurable and is physical.  As $|\mathbf{k}|$ grows, both
$k_{z,P}$ and $k_{z,S}$ tend to $i|\mathbf{k}|$, so the $P$ and $S$ columns of
$\mathbf{D}_z$ become parallel: $\cos(+P,+S)$ reaches $1.000000$, and
$\mathrm{cond}(\mathbf{D}_z)$ grows as $|\mathbf{k}|^{2.001}$ \emph{after} the
symplectic row scaling has been applied.  This is the elastostatic degeneracy
--- at short lateral scale there is no distinction between $P$ and $S$, both
being the same decaying static field --- and unlike the unit mismatch of
\S\ref{sec:thesisspectral}, which the row scaling does remove, it cannot be
scaled away.  Up- and downgoing modes are untouched: $\cos(+P,-P)$ falls to
$2\times10^{-6}$.

\paragraph{The ceiling this imposes.}  At $|\mathbf{k}| = 600$,
$\bV_{du}$ and $\bV_{ud}$ are each $2.0\times10^{6}$ while the eigenvalues of
their product are only $79^2$ --- a nine-order cancellation.  The similarity
$\mathrm{eig}(\bV) = \mathrm{eig}(\dA)$, which is exact in theory, degrades
accordingly: $1.5\times10^{-15}$, $4.2\times10^{-13}$, $3.5\times10^{-8}$,
$2.0$ at $|\mathbf{k}| = 2,\,20,\,200,\,600$.  \textbf{The mode basis is sound to
$|\mathbf{k}|\approx200$ here, about $35$ times the $S$ wavenumber, and not
beyond.}  Whether that is a real limit depends on the lateral resolution the
scatterer demands, $|\mathbf{k}|_{\max}\sim\pi N/a$, and so it is recorded as a
range rather than dismissed.

\paragraph{What it suggests.}  Where the modes coalesce, the mode basis is the
wrong basis, and the remedy is not to condition $\bV$ but to avoid forming it:
march an impedance in the physical $(\bu,\bta_3)$ variables, where
$\mathbf{D}_z$ never appears and the coefficient matrices are the blocks of
$\bA + \dA$ themselves, polynomial in $\mathbf{k}$.  \S\ref{sec:impedance}
builds it and finds that it does clear the ceiling.

\subsection{The impedance form, and the ceiling cleared}\label{sec:impedance}

\begin{quote}
\small\itshape
A correction, twice over.  An earlier draft of the paragraph above asserted that
marching an impedance ``is what the existing Riccati solver and the classical
imbedding codes do''.  The survey that preceded this subsection found otherwise:
no matrix impedance exists in this package, in the sibling repository, or in the
thesis.  What exists is a \emph{scalar} $SH$ impedance used as an eigenvector
normalisation convention, and two Riccati sweeps --- both of which march a
reflection matrix in \emph{mode amplitudes}.  That much stands.

The draft then concluded ``the construction below is new'', and \emph{that} is
wrong.  The survey covered this package, the sibling repository, the thesis and
the symbolic archive; it did not cover the literature, and the literature has
it.  Haines, Hulme \& Yu (2004) give exactly this construction --- an impedance
operator satisfying a Riccati equation, solved as an initial value problem,
together with a one-way equation for the particle velocity --- and handle
lateral heterogeneity by expanding in a truncated Fourier basis, which turns
their impedance operator into a finite matrix.  That is the same object as
$\bY$ below and the same means of carrying the lateral coupling inside it.
Thomson (2015a,b) then established the symmetry of that operator, which is the
identity used here as the main structural check.  \S\ref{sec:priorart} sets out
the correspondence term by term and says what, if anything, is left over.
\end{quote}

Split $\bq = (\bu,\bta_3)$ and $\bA$ into $3\times3$ blocks, and let
$\bta_3 = \bY\bu$.  Then $\bu' = (\bA_{11}+\bA_{12}\bY)\bu$ and
$\bta_3' = (\bA_{21}+\bA_{22}\bY)\bu$, while $\bta_3' = \bY'\bu + \bY\bu'$, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:impedance}
  \bY' = \bA_{21} + \bA_{22}\bY - \bY\bA_{11} - \bY\bA_{12}\bY .
\end{equation}
The coefficients are the blocks of $\bA$ itself, polynomial in $\mathbf{k}$,
with no eigenvectors anywhere.  In a homogeneous medium $\bY'=0$ and
\eqref{eq:impedance} becomes an algebraic Riccati equation whose stabilising
solution is the downgoing half-space impedance; the equation is confirmed by
checking that the impedance read off $\mathbf{D}_z$ satisfies it, to
$10^{-14}$, where $\mathbf{D}_z$ is still trustworthy.

\paragraph{Why it behaves differently, in one line.}  $\bV$ needs the
\emph{full} inverse $\mathbf{D}_z^{-1}$, which mixes up- and downgoing and is
where the cancellation lives.  $\bY = \mathbf{T}_d\mathbf{U}_d^{-1}$ needs only
the three \emph{downgoing} columns.  Measured side by side over
$|\mathbf{k}| = 2$ to $6000$: the impedance residual tracks
$\mathrm{cond}(\mathbf{U}_d)\,\epsilon$ linearly, reaching
$7.2\times10^{-10}$, while $\bV$ returns $2.000$ from
$|\mathbf{k}| = 600$ on.  That $2.000$ is $|{-}ik_z - ik_z|/|k_z|$: a lost
branch, not a lost digit.

\paragraph{Refining with $\bA$ alone.}  Newton's method on
\eqref{eq:impedance} --- the correction solving a Sylvester equation built from
$\bA$ --- holds the residual at $10^{-16}$ at \emph{every} wavenumber tested.
The division of labour is the natural one: $\mathbf{D}_z$ is analytic, so it
names the right branch however imprecise it has become, and $\bA$ supplies the
precision.  Continuation in $|\mathbf{k}|$ was tried instead of a
$\mathbf{D}_z$ start and \emph{flipped branch} at $\omega/\alpha$, returning the
upgoing root; the arbiter caught it at exactly $2.000$.  The branch is then
confirmed without reference to $\mathbf{D}_z$ at all, by requiring every
eigenvalue of $\bA_{11}+\bA_{12}\bY$ to have negative real part.

\paragraph{$\bY$ is bounded, where $\bV$ was not.}  This is the whole
difference.  $\|\bY\|/\mu|\mathbf{k}|$ converges to $1.529022$ and
$\mathrm{cond}(\bY)$ to $2.0000$ across four decades --- the elastostatic
half-space stiffness, $O(1)$ conditioning, no growth relative to its own
spectrum.  The limit is also $\omega$-independent, as a static limit must be:
two frequencies a factor of four apart agree to $7.9\times10^{-6}$ at
$|\mathbf{k}| = 6000$, converging as $|\mathbf{k}|^{-2}$.

\paragraph{The arbiter, and the verdict.}  Self-consistency would not settle
this, so $\bY$ is scored against an independently known analytic quantity:
$\mathrm{eig}(\bA_{11}+\bA_{12}\bY)$ must be $\{ik_{z,P},ik_{z,S},ik_{z,S}\}$
with $k_{z,c}$ from (kzcDef) in closed form.  The impedance holds it to
$3.4\times10^{-10}$ at $|\mathbf{k}| = 6000$, drifting as about
$|\mathbf{k}|^{1.6}$ from $2\times10^{-13}$ at $|\mathbf{k}| = 60$.  It is not
exact.  What it does not do is fail.  \textbf{The usable range goes from
$|\mathbf{k}|\approx200$ to beyond $6000$ --- a factor of thirty --- and the
failure mode changes from losing the branch to losing digits slowly.}

\subsection{What $\bY$ is: the Dirichlet-to-Neumann
map}\label{sec:dtn}

Prescribe the displacement $\bu$ on the plane $x_3=z$ --- Dirichlet data ---
solve the field equations in the region below it subject to the radiation
condition at depth, and read off the traction $\bta_3$ that results --- Neumann
data.  The operator carrying the one to the other is the Dirichlet-to-Neumann
map of that region, and it is $\bY$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dtn}
  \bta_3 = \bY(z)\,\bu ,
\end{equation}
so $\bY(z)$ is the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map of the half-space below $z$.

This is not a gloss on \eqref{eq:impedance}; it is what \eqref{eq:impedance} is
an equation \emph{for}.  It is also the single idea the rest of this note keeps
meeting under different names --- the imbedding equation, the impedance form,
the M\"obius step, the symplectic identity, the boundedness of $\bY$ where $\bV$
was unbounded, the dominance of the quadratic term --- and each of those is a
consequence of \eqref{eq:dtn} rather than an independent finding.  The purpose
of this subsection is to say so once, carefully, and to record which of the
consequences are measured.  They are gated in
\verb|gate_first_order_lateral_impedance_3d.py|, part~14.

\paragraph{The scalar case, which is the whole idea.}  Take one dimension, an
acoustic medium of density $\rho$ and speed $c$, modulus $M=\rho c^{2}$.  The
first-order system is $u'=\tau/M$, $\tau'=-\rho\omega^{2}u$, so
$A_{11}=A_{22}=0$, $A_{12}=1/M$, $A_{21}=-\rho\omega^{2}$.  In a half-space
$Y'=0$ and \eqref{eq:impedance} collapses to the algebraic statement
\begin{equation}\label{eq:scalarare}
  0 = -\rho\omega^{2} - \frac{Y^{2}}{M}
  \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
  Y = \pm\, i\omega\sqrt{\rho M} = \pm\, i\omega\rho c ,
\end{equation}
and the radiation condition picks the sign.  So $Y$ \emph{is} the acoustic
impedance $\rho c$, carrying the factor $i\omega$ that converts displacement to
velocity; ``impedance operator'' is not an analogy.  The elastic matrix reduces
to exactly this: at normal incidence the three channels decouple and
$\mathrm{diag}(\bY)=i\omega\rho\,(\alpha,\beta,\beta)$ to $10^{-13}$, with the
off-diagonal at $10^{-16}$ of the whole.

Two media in welded contact share both $\bu$ and $\bta_3$, so $\bY$ is
\emph{continuous} across an interface --- which is the entire content of the
interface condition, and is why reflection coefficients fall out of it.  Writing
$\bu = \bU_d\mathbf{a}_d + \bU_u\mathbf{a}_u$ and imposing \eqref{eq:dtn},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rfromy}
  \bR = -\bigl(\mathbf{T}_u - \bY\,\bU_u\bigr)^{-1}
         \bigl(\mathbf{T}_d - \bY\,\bU_d\bigr)
      = \bU_u^{-1}\,(\bY_u-\bY)^{-1}(\bY-\bY_d)\,\bU_d ,
\end{equation}
which in the scalar case with $\bY_{u,d}=\mp i\omega\rho_1c_1$ and
$\bY=i\omega\rho_2c_2$ is $|R| = |Z_2-Z_1|/(Z_2+Z_1)$, $Z=\rho c$ --- the
textbook coefficient, and the identity the impedance-null test of
\S\ref{sec:threed} rests on.  \emph{This is the first place the advantage shows.}
Equation~\eqref{eq:rfromy} says $\bR$ is $\bY$ read in a chart, and reaching that
chart costs the mode matrices $\bU_u,\bU_d$ --- that is, $\mathbf{D}_z$ and its
inverse.  $\bY$ needs no chart at all.

\paragraph{Existence, which is the argument \cite{haines2004} actually make.}
Displacement and traction are physical fields; they exist whatever the medium
does, and so does the map between them.  An up/down decomposition does not: it
presupposes a mode structure that, in \cite{haines2004}'s words, ``can become
poorly defined for laterally varying media''.  Their objection is about
existence, not about conditioning, and \eqref{eq:dtn} is what answers it.  The
conditioning argument of \S\ref{sec:impedance} is separate and is this note's
own; \eqref{eq:rfromy} shows the two are related --- the chart that may fail to
exist is the same one that is ill-conditioned when it does.

\paragraph{The transformation law is M\"obius, and the march applies it.}  Let
$\bM=e^{-\bA h}$ propagate $\bq=(\bu,\bta_3)$ up through a uniform step.  The
graph of $\bY_{\text{bot}}$ is carried to the graph of
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mobius}
  \bY_{\text{top}} = \bigl(\bM_{21}+\bM_{22}\bY_{\text{bot}}\bigr)
                     \bigl(\bM_{11}+\bM_{12}\bY_{\text{bot}}\bigr)^{-1},
  \qquad \bM = e^{-\bA h},
\end{equation}
a matrix fractional-linear --- M\"obius --- transformation.  The
\texttt{mobius} method of the march is literally one application of
\eqref{eq:mobius}, which is why it is exact for a piecewise-constant medium and
why it is therefore a layer stack.  In a uniform medium the
half-space map is the \emph{fixed point} of this action, and that is how
\eqref{eq:mobius} is checked here: to $9.2\times10^{-16}$ at $h=5$ and
$3.3\times10^{-15}$ at $h=80$, with the propagator that acts symplectic to
$4\times10^{-8}$.

The Riccati equation is the infinitesimal form of \eqref{eq:mobius}.  Put
$\bM=\mathbf{I}-\bA h+O(h^{2})$, expand, and the $O(h)$ term is
\eqref{eq:impedance}.  So ``imbed the problem in the family indexed by the
position of the surface and differentiate along the family'' --- the move of
\cite{bellman1959} and \cite{mccoy1986}, and the imbedding step of
\S\ref{sec:imbedding} --- and ``write down the M\"obius action of the propagator
and take its generator'' are the same derivation.

\paragraph{Which way it marches, and why there are two directions.}  The
question is natural and the answer is not a convention.  $\bY(z)$ maps what lies
\emph{below} $z$, so the one depth at which it is known in advance is the
\textbf{base}, where the radiation condition supplies the half-space impedance
of \S\ref{sec:impedance}.  At the surface $\bY$ is the answer, not the input.
The Riccati equation therefore runs \textbf{upward}, from the base to the
surface, and Algorithm~1 is written as an upsweep for that reason rather than
for a stylistic one.

Stability then agrees, and does so for a reason worth isolating.  Perturbing the
half-space impedance and integrating \eqref{eq:impedance} both ways over one
slab thickness, the perturbation is $0.92$ of its initial size marching towards
the surface and $3.2\times10^{3}$ marching towards depth.  \textbf{But that
asymmetry is carried entirely by the evanescent channels.}  Where every lateral
mode propagates, $\mathrm{eig}(\bA_{11}+\bA_{12}\bY)$ is purely imaginary --- to
$10^{-17}$ --- the fixed point is neutral, and neither direction is preferred.
A direction test run on a grid too coarse to reach $k_P$ measures nothing, which
is how this one first came out inconclusive.

The second direction is real, and belongs to the other pass.  Once $\bY$ is
known, the field is recovered from the one-way equation
$\bu'=(\bA_{11}+\bA_{12}\bY)\bu$ integrated \textbf{downward} from the surface,
in the direction of energy flow --- the structure \cite{haines2004} state
explicitly and which \S\ref{sec:priorart} records.  \emph{The Riccati runs
against the energy flow and the field runs with it}, and neither is a choice.

A third object, easily confused with the first, genuinely does march downward
from the top: the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map of the region \emph{above} $z$,
started from $\bY=\mathbf{0}$ at a traction-free surface.  That is the operator
for a problem whose radiation condition sits on the other side --- a source
beneath the structure rather than above it --- and it is not the one marched
here.

\paragraph{The graph is Lagrangian, and that is reciprocity.}  Set
$\mathbf{W}=\begin{psmallmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{3N}\\ \bY\end{psmallmatrix}$, whose
columns span the downgoing subspace.  Restricting the symplectic form to it,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lagrangian}
  \mathbf{W}\tp\Jsix\mathbf{W} = \bY-\bY\tp,
\end{equation}
so \emph{$\bY$ symmetric} and \emph{the downgoing subspace is Lagrangian} are
one statement.  Since $\Jsix\bA=(\Jsix\bA)\tp$ makes the flow symplectic, and a
symplectic flow carries Lagrangian subspaces to Lagrangian subspaces, symmetry
is \textbf{preserved by the march rather than approached by it}.  The
measurement says exactly that: $\|\bY-\bY\tp\|/\|\bY\| \le 2.7\times10^{-16}$
at every contrast and every step count tried, with \emph{no} improvement under
refinement, because there is nothing to improve.  Physically it is reciprocity;
the symplectic identity used throughout this note as a discretisation check is
this same fact one level down.

This also says what $\bY$ is as a geometric object: a point of the
\emph{Lagrangian Grassmannian}, the space of Lagrangian subspaces, of which
symmetric matrices are one coordinate chart.

\paragraph{Why $\bY$ is bounded where $\bV$ was not.}  The Grassmannian is
compact; the chart is not.  $\bY$ blows up exactly where its graph stops being
transverse to $\{\bu=0\}$, and $\bR$ blows up --- by \eqref{eq:rfromy} --- where
$\bY_u-\bY$ becomes singular.  \textbf{Both are chart singularities, not
singularities of the field}, and they sit in different places.  That is the
structural reason behind the measurement of \S\ref{sec:impedance}: $\bV$
returning $2.000$ from $|\mathbf{k}|=600$ is the reflection chart failing, not
the solution failing.

Whether the $\bY$ chart fails is then a physical question with a physical
answer.  $\bY$ becomes singular only if the region below has a resonance with a
\emph{clamped} surface, and a region radiating into a half-space at real
$\omega$ has none, because energy leaks out of every candidate mode.  That is an
argument rather than a proof, and it is offered as one; what supports it is the
measurement already reported --- $\|\bY\|/\mu|\mathbf{k}|\to1.529022$ and
$\mathrm{cond}(\bY)\to2.0000$ across four decades, with no growth anywhere.

\paragraph{Energy: the anti-Hermitian part, and what its kernel counts.}  A
Dirichlet-to-Neumann surface can lose energy downward but cannot be fed from
below, so $(\bY-\bY^{\dagger})/2i$ must be positive \emph{semi}-definite.  Semi,
not definite: an evanescent channel carries no flux and contributes an exact
zero, so the kernel is not slack in the statement but a count.  One zero per
P-evanescent lateral mode of the half-space below, two per S-evanescent one.  At
the gate's parameters ($k_P=0.0120$, $k_S=0.0200$, $\max|\mathbf{k}|=0.0178$)
that predicts $20$ null channels of $108$, and $20$ is what is measured --- with
the most negative eigenvalue at $-5.5\times10^{-16}$ of scale and a gap of
$0.24$ of scale to the smallest surviving one, so the count is unambiguous.

Two things are worth drawing out.  The prediction is a \emph{count}, which no
tolerance can be tuned to satisfy; and the regime it counts is precisely the one
\cite{haines2004} single out as awkward --- wavenumbers at which $P$ has gone
evanescent while $S$ still propagates.  Every one of the $20$ is such a channel.
The construction meets that case by not distinguishing it.

\paragraph{A Schur complement, which is why the quadratic term is irreducible.}
Discretise the region below the surface and eliminate every interior degree of
freedom.  With $\mathbf{K}$ the stiffness, $b$ the boundary and $i$ the interior,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:schur}
  \bY = \mathbf{K}_{bb} - \mathbf{K}_{bi}\mathbf{K}_{ii}^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{ib},
\end{equation}
which is static condensation of an entire half-space onto one surface, and
$\bY\bA_{12}\bY$ in \eqref{eq:impedance} is
$\mathbf{K}_{bi}\mathbf{K}_{ii}^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{ib}$ in differential form.  This
settles structurally what \S\ref{sec:latcost} measures.  That term is $69\%$ of
the step and is dense in \emph{every} basis, because the Schur complement of a
connected region is dense however the region is parametrised --- so no change of
basis reaches it.  It is also, for the same reason, the term that carries the
multiple scattering: it is the only place where what is below the surface acts
back on it.

\paragraph{The exact transparent boundary condition.}  A Dirichlet-to-Neumann
map is a relation on a surface that replaces an infinite domain with no
approximation whatever.  An absorbing layer is an approximation to this object;
here the object itself is in hand, which is why the slab is closed at its base
by $\bY$ rather than by a sponge, and why a vertical absorbing layer never
arises as a question.  It arises laterally, where the grid is periodic and no
such map is available --- and an absorbing layer tried there is the one part of
this construction that failed to earn its place, recorded in the gate rather
than here because it produced no result to report.

\paragraph{$\bY$ is a complete state.}  Nothing below the surface enters except
through \eqref{eq:dtn}, so the march carries no history.  Stopping it at
mid-slab and restarting from the value reached returns the same answer: to the
arithmetic floor when the two stages use the same step size --- it is then the
same arithmetic in the same order --- and to $1.4\times10^{-7}$ when they do
not, which is truncation error and not a discrepancy.  The practical consequence
is that regions compose by handing over one matrix, and the march exposes this
as a starting value rather than hiding it.  It is also, in a second guise, the
reuse of \S\ref{sec:tricks}: $\bY$ contains no reference to a source, so one
march serves any number of them.

\paragraph{Its other names.}  The same operator is the \emph{admittance} or
\emph{impedance operator} of \cite{haines1989,hainesdehoop1996,haines2004}, the
\emph{static condensation} of structural analysis, the \emph{transparent} or
non-reflecting boundary condition of numerical wave propagation, and --- in one
dimension --- the Weyl--Titchmarsh $m$-function.  Whether the map determines the
medium is the Calder\'on problem, whose answer is that it does.  That is worth
recording because it identifies what the march computes as exactly the object an
inverse problem would want to measure, although nothing in this note pursues it.

\paragraph{What it does not give, and the second sweep that supplies it.}
The map is a statement on one surface: it carries no information about the field
in the interior.  Reflection is therefore available from $\bY$ at the top alone,
by \eqref{eq:rfromy}, but \emph{transmission is not} --- transmission is the
field carried through the slab, and recovering it needs the one-way equation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:oneway}
  \bu' = \bigl(\bA_{11}+\bA_{12}\bY(z)\bigr)\bu ,
\end{equation}
integrated \textbf{downward} from the surface while the Riccati runs upward
against it.  That is the double sweep of \cite{haines2004}, and it is now built:
\S\ref{sec:rt} reports it.  It costs one thing the single sweep did not need ---
$\bY$ at every depth rather than only at the top --- which is why the upward
sweep is run at twice the resolution and every value kept, so that the downward
Runge--Kutta finds $\bY$ already evaluated at each of its stage depths and no
interpolation enters.

Two limitations are unaffected by it.  The map is frequency-by-frequency, hence
non-local in time, so nothing here transfers to a time-domain march without an
additional construction.  And \cite{haines2004} reuse \emph{fundamental
solutions} as well as the operator; the second sweep is the machinery those are
built from, but assembling and reusing them is not done here.

\subsection{The reflection/transmission response, from both
sweeps}\label{sec:rt}

Reflection needs one sweep and transmission needs two, so the second sweep is
what turns an impedance into a scattering response.  With unit downgoing
amplitude above the slab the total field at the top is
$\bu(0)=(\bU_d+\bU_u\bR)$, and sweeping \eqref{eq:oneway} down to the base --
where the field is purely downgoing -- gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tfromsweep}
  \bT = \bU_d^{-1}\,\bu(2a) .
\end{equation}

\paragraph{The exact limit, first.}  At zero contrast every entry is known in
closed form, which tests the sweep before any arbiter is involved: $\bR$
vanishes to $10^{-15}$, $\bT$ is diagonal to $7\times10^{-16}$ --- a uniform
slab mixes no modes --- and $\bT$ approaches
$\mathrm{diag}\,e^{ik_{z,c}\cdot2a}$ at \emph{fourth} order, $3.96$, $4.00$,
$4.00$ over $4$ to $32$ steps.  The downward sweep therefore carries the same
order as the upward one, which is what the stage-matched $\bY$ history is for.

every incident channel, every outgoing channel, $P$, $SV$ and $SH$ alike, and
every propagating diffraction order.  Rescaling to the flux basis by

\paragraph{$S$ incidence, scored.}  The comparison above is the $P$ column,

One entry is a symmetry rather than a measurement and is worth keeping: the

\paragraph{What is still open, and it is the arbiter again.}  An $x$-polarised
$S$ wave excites the \emph{toroidal} family as well as the spheroidal one, and
\texttt{compute\_elastic\_mie} carries its coefficients.  What is missing is the
scalar relating them to the shared potential convention: they are normalised
against their own incident expansion, which the spheroidal coefficients do not
share, and the package's $SH$ far field cannot settle it because that routine
drops the toroidal part outright as ``$O((ka)^4)$ and negligible'' --- a claim
that fails at $k_Sa=2.4$, where the toroidal coefficients run $1.5$ to $2.5$
times the spheroidal ones.  Scanning the scalar against the march \emph{bounds}
it at or below $k_S$, where the toroidal is invisible against the array floor,
and rules out $1$ (median agreement $0.94\to0.58$) and $1/(ik_S)$ (which
destroys it).  Bounded, not determined, and it is recorded that way rather than
fitted: a scalar tuned against the one comparison it is scored on always looks
successful.

\subsection{The symplectic picture, and what it
guarantees}\label{sec:symplectic}

The identity $\Jsix\bA=(\Jsix\bA)\tp$ has been used three times already in this
note --- as a discretisation check in \S\ref{sec:symrel}, as the reason the
lateral operator must be derived rather than read off in \S\ref{sec:latop}, and
as the source of $\bY=\bY\tp$ in \S\ref{sec:dtn}.  They are one structure, and
it is worth setting out on its own, because it is what makes several of this
note's results exact rather than approximate.

\paragraph{The algebra and the group.}  Written as $\bA\tp\Jsix = -\Jsix\bA$,
the identity is exactly the defining condition of the symplectic Lie algebra
$\mathfrak{sp}(6N)$.  Its exponential therefore lies in the symplectic group:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sympgroup}
  \bM = e^{-\bA h} \;\in\; \mathrm{Sp}(6N),
  \qquad \bM\tp\Jsix\bM = \Jsix ,
\end{equation}
measured here to $4\times10^{-8}$ at $h=5$ and $5\times10^{-7}$ at $h=80$ --- a
looser figure than the algebraic identity behind it, which holds to
$8.7\times10^{-17}$, because it is the accuracy of the matrix exponential rather
than of the structure.  Physically the conserved form is the vertical power
flux: $\Jsix$ pairs $-\bta_3$ with $\bv$, which \S\ref{sec:metric} chose for
exactly that reason, so \eqref{eq:sympgroup} is energy flux conservation written
as a group property.

\paragraph{Lagrangian subspaces.}  A $3N$-dimensional subspace $L$ of the $6N$-%
dimensional phase space is \emph{Lagrangian} when the symplectic form vanishes
identically on it --- the largest dimension at which that is possible.  The
downgoing solutions span such a subspace, and \eqref{eq:lagrangian} is the
statement in coordinates: for the graph $\mathbf{W}=(\mathbf{I},\bY)\tp$,
$\mathbf{W}\tp\Jsix\mathbf{W}=\bY-\bY\tp$, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lagequiv}
  \bY \text{ symmetric}
  \iff
  \text{the downgoing subspace is Lagrangian}
  \iff
  \text{reciprocity.}
\end{equation}
The three are the same fact in three languages, and the middle one is the
reason the outer two are exact.

\paragraph{The Lagrangian Grassmannian.}  Collect all such subspaces and the
result is a compact manifold $\Lambda(3N)$ of dimension $3N(3N{+}1)/2$, on which
$\mathrm{Sp}(6N)$ acts.  Symmetric matrices are \emph{one coordinate chart} on
it, and \eqref{eq:mobius} is that action written in the chart --- which is why
the transformation law is fractional-linear and why its generator is a Riccati
equation.  Every object this note marches is a curve in $\Lambda(3N)$; $\bY$ and
$\bR$ are two ways of writing down where that curve is.

\paragraph{What this buys: reciprocity is exact, and needs no symplectic
integrator.}  This is the part worth stating carefully, because the obvious
inference is wrong.  One might expect that preserving $\bY=\bY\tp$ requires a
structure-preserving integrator --- and it does not.  Write the right-hand side
of \eqref{eq:impedance} as $\mathcal{R}(\bY)$.  The symplectic identity makes
$\bA_{21}$ and $\bA_{12}$ symmetric and $\bA_{22}=-\bA_{11}\tp$, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rhssym}
  \mathcal{R}(\bY)\tp
  = \bA_{21} + \bY\tp\bA_{22}\tp - \bA_{11}\tp\bY\tp - \bY\tp\bA_{12}\bY\tp
  = \mathcal{R}(\bY\tp),
\end{equation}
so $\mathcal{R}$ carries symmetric matrices to symmetric matrices.  A
Runge--Kutta step is a linear combination of evaluations of $\mathcal{R}$ added
to $\bY$, and linear combinations of symmetric matrices are symmetric.
\textbf{Symmetry is therefore preserved exactly by any explicit Runge--Kutta
method, not merely to the order of the method}, and the measurement shows the
signature: $\|\bY-\bY\tp\|/\|\bY\| \le 2.7\times10^{-16}$, unchanged between $8$
and $32$ steps and between zero and six per cent contrast.  A quantity that is
being approximated improves under refinement; this one does not move.  It is
also why the M\"obius step and RK4 agree on reciprocity although only the former
is symplectic.

\paragraph{What this buys: a discretisation test with a control.}  The identity
belongs to the continuum operator, and a discretisation inherits it only if it
is built correctly --- which makes it the sharpest available check on the
lateral operator.  \S\ref{sec:latop} derives the traction rows with each
derivative \emph{outside} its modulus, and that is what the identity is
testing: the derived ordering gives $8.7\times10^{-17}$, the plausible wrong
ordering with the modulus pulled outside gives $1.3\times10^{-1}$, and the two
are indistinguishable to $1.3\times10^{-17}$ in a laterally uniform medium.  A
check that cannot fail proves nothing, so the control is the point: this
identity is the only test in the note that distinguishes the two orderings at
all.

\paragraph{Where the charts fail.}  The subspaces that are \emph{not} transverse
to the reference one form the Maslov cycle of $\Lambda(3N)$, and that is exactly
the set where the $\bY$ chart blows up; $\bR$ blows up on a different cycle,
which is \S\ref{sec:dtn}'s point that the two fail in different places.  Since
$\Lambda(3N)$ is compact and the charts are not, some such failure is
unavoidable for any coordinate --- the question is only whether the physical
problem drives the curve into that particular cycle, and \S\ref{sec:dtn} argues
on radiation grounds that for $\bY$ it does not.  Counting crossings is the
Maslov index, which in one dimension is the oscillation theory of Sturm and
Liouville; nothing here uses it, and it is named only so that the structure is
recognisable.

\subsection{Marching it through a slab, and scoring
it}\label{sec:impmarch}

\S\ref{sec:impedance} only ever solved the \emph{algebraic} Riccati equation, in
a homogeneous medium.  This is the march: integrate \eqref{eq:impedance} upward
from the base of a slab, starting from the downgoing impedance of the background
half-space --- the radiation condition, and the continuum form of
$\mathbf{Y}_{M+1}=0$ --- then read a reflection matrix off the result using
continuity of $\bu$ and $\bta_3$ and nothing else --- equation
\eqref{eq:rfromy}, which \S\ref{sec:dtn} reads as the statement that $\bR$ is
$\bY$ expressed in a chart that costs a mode decomposition.

Two integrators are carried so that each checks the other.  For a
piecewise-constant medium the step is the \emph{linear-fractional transform}
\eqref{eq:mobius} and is exact, so one step settles a uniform slab outright; RK4 applied to
\eqref{eq:impedance} directly never forms $e^{-\bA h}$ at all, and is the
control.

\paragraph{Absolute, before anything relative.}  At normal incidence one step of
\eqref{eq:mobius} reproduces the closed form to $1.4\times10^{-16}$,
$4.5\times10^{-16}$ and $3.8\times10^{-15}$ at contrasts of $+2\%$, $+10\%$ and
$+40\%$ --- with no discretisation in it anywhere.  RK4 then converges on the
same answer at ratios $11.4,\,15.3,\,15.9,\,15.9$: fourth order, confirming that
\eqref{eq:impedance} is what is being integrated.

\paragraph{Against Kennett.}  The thesis and Kennett amplitude conventions
differ, and \S\ref{sec:whatconvention} determines exactly how; for the moment
what is compared is the three \emph{diagonal} ratios, which must be constant
because a convention cannot depend on angle.  Across $\sin i = 0$ to $0.75$ they
are constant to $4.6\times10^{-14}$, and equal
$(-1,-1,+1)$ to $4.8\times10^{-14}$.  \textbf{The $SH$ sign does not flip where
$P$--$SV$ does}; the split is real, and lumping the three together is what made
this gate's first run report a spurious $2.000$.  The collocation route of
\S\ref{sec:layer} reaches the same three constants, $(-1.000042,\,-1.000166,\,
+1.000166)$ at $120$ cells, by an entirely different path --- so this is a
cross-check between two independent routes rather than a fitted offset, and the
march reaches it ten orders more sharply.  The constants survive past both
critical slownesses, where the march has to carry evanescence.

\paragraph{The graded slab, which is the point.}  A uniform slab never needed a
march.  Grade the contrast with depth and no closed form exists --- while
Kennett, cut at the \emph{same} depths, is solving the identical
piecewise-constant problem.  The two must therefore agree to the arithmetic
floor at finite step count, not merely converge together, and they do:
$3.6\times10^{-15}$, $2.2\times10^{-15}$ and $1.1\times10^{-14}$ at $4$, $16$
and $64$ steps, at normal and oblique incidence alike.  Reversing the depth
profile --- same layers, same thicknesses, reordered --- changes $\bR$ by
$50\%$, so the agreement is not the insensitivity of a march that cannot see
where the contrast sits.

\subsection{What the convention actually is}\label{sec:whatconvention}

\begin{quote}
\small\itshape
A correction, and a instructive one.  Both this section and \S\ref{sec:layer}
previously said the two conventions ``differ by a fixed matrix''.  That was
asserted on the strength of the three \emph{diagonal} ratios, which cannot
establish it --- and it is wrong twice: the relation is two-sided rather than a
single matrix, and it is not fixed.
\end{quote}

A convention is a renormalisation of mode amplitudes, and the incident and
reflected sets need not be renormalised the same way.  If
$\mathbf{a}_d^{\text{th}} = \bS_d\,\mathbf{a}_d^{\text{ken}}$ and
$\mathbf{a}_u^{\text{th}} = \bS_u\,\mathbf{a}_u^{\text{ken}}$ then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:convention}
  \bR_{\text{th}} = \bS_u\,\bR_{\text{ken}}\,\bS_d^{-1},
\end{equation}
which is a \emph{two-sided} transform.  If both are diagonal, the elementwise
ratio $Q_{ij} = (\bR_{\text{th}})_{ij}/(\bR_{\text{ken}})_{ij}$ satisfies
$Q_{ij} = (\bS_u)_i/(\bS_d)_j$ --- rank one in log space, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rankone}
  Q_{PP}\,Q_{SS} = Q_{PS}\,Q_{SP}
\end{equation}
exactly.  That is a sharp test of diagonality, and the diagonal-only measurement
never performed it.  Measured, \eqref{eq:rankone} holds to $1.9\times10^{-14}$:
both renormalisations \emph{are} diagonal.

Writing $Q_{ij} = -s_i/s_j$ with $s = (1,\,ic)$, the off-diagonals give $c$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:convratio}
  c = \sqrt{k_{z,S}/k_{z,P}}\,,
\end{equation}
to $5.6\times10^{-15}$ across the sweep.  This is the displacement-versus-flux
difference: Kennett normalises modes to unit vertical energy flux, which carries
$\sqrt{k_z}$ per mode, while the columns here are normalised by their own
displacement component.  \textbf{$c$ runs from $1.293$ to $2.466$ over
$p = 2\times10^{-5}$ to $1.95\times10^{-4}$ and diverges as
$k_{z,P}\to0$ at the $P$ critical slowness, so the convention is not fixed.}
What is fixed is its diagonal --- $\bS_u = -\bS_d$ on $P$--$SV$ and
$\bS_u = +\bS_d$ on $SH$, giving $(-1,-1,+1)$ --- which is all
\S\ref{sec:impmarch} and \S\ref{sec:layer} ever saw.

Two further things are worth stating precisely.  The overall scale is not
observable at all: \eqref{eq:convention} is unchanged by
$\bS_{u,d}\to\lambda\bS_{u,d}$ on both sides, so only $\bS_u\bS_d^{-1}$ and the $P/S$ ratio
within each set are determined --- reporting a single convention matrix would
be overclaiming a third time.  And the standing signature of a
displacement-versus-flux difference is that it is \emph{invisible to any
per-mode ratio test}, which is precisely the test that was being run.

\subsection{What this is, against the thesis's own
development}\label{sec:impedanceplace}

It is worth being exact about which part of this is new, because most of it is
not, and the parts that are not are the load-bearing ones.

\paragraph{The problem the thesis set.}  The target is laterally varying
propagation in which \emph{the dominant material variation is in depth}.  That is
not a simplification of convenience: it is the structure of the medium.
Depth-average properties generally increase with depth, so the reference carries
a strong gradient, while the lateral departure from that depth-average is
superimposed and comparatively weak.  The thesis's two-potential split is built
on exactly that asymmetry --- the depth-dependent profile is solved
\emph{exactly and never expanded}, and only the lateral contrast is carried as a
series --- and, because the contrast is a deviation from the depth-average at
each depth, it has zero lateral mean by construction, so the reference is the
optimal expansion point and not merely a convenient one.

\paragraph{The thesis already says this is the regime for a Riccati sweep.}
Its Introduction states the criterion directly: the Riccati sweep ``factorizes
the reference medium Green's tensor, but does not include any of the scattered
field in the reference solution.  Hence, a multiple scattering series based on
this scheme converges rapidly, \emph{even when there are strong gradients in the
reference model}, provided that the superimposed heterogeneity is not too
strong.''  Strong gradients in the reference, weak superimposed heterogeneity:
that is the stated regime, and it is the regime above.  The complementary case
--- smooth reference, strong heterogeneity --- the thesis assigns to its
cumulative forward-scattering factorisation instead, so the choice between them
is already made and is not being re-opened here.

\paragraph{What is therefore borrowed.}  The formulation, the regime, the
criterion, the vertical operator and the economy that makes it affordable are
all the thesis's \cite{nestor1996}: the block-tridiagonal structure
(eq.~\texttt{Amat}), the Riccati factorisation and Algorithm~5.1, the
$\bP^{z}$ vertical sweep, and the observation that
$Q^{\partial} = (\bI - \mathcal{S}_{int}\mathcal{E})^{-1}\mathcal{S}_{int}$
depends on the reference alone and so is built \emph{once} per frequency, after
which each Krylov iterate is a cheap forward--backward sweep.  Nothing here
displaces any of that.

\paragraph{What is new, and it is two things.}  First, the marched variable.
The thesis factorises in reflection/transmission operators on mode amplitudes;
\S\ref{sec:nonnormal} measures why that has a ceiling --- $P$ and $S$ coalesce
at large lateral wavenumber, $\mathrm{cond}(\mathbf{D}_z)$ grows as
$|\mathbf{k}|^2$, and the mode-basis coefficients lose the \emph{branch} by
$|\mathbf{k}| = 600$ --- and \S\ref{sec:impedance} shows the impedance is free
of it, because $\bY$ needs only the downgoing columns and never the full
inverse.  This is not academic for the present problem: resolving lateral
structure of scale $a$ on $N$ points demands
$|\mathbf{k}|_{\max}\sim\pi N/a$, so the ceiling is reached by refining the
lateral mesh, which is precisely what a laterally varying calculation does.

Second, the march is a \emph{continuum} one.  The thesis factorises a stack of
interfaces; \eqref{eq:impedance} is an ODE in $z$.  For a stack the two agree
exactly --- \S\ref{sec:impmarch} measures agreement with Kennett to
$10^{-14}$ on a graded stack cut at the same depths --- but for a
\emph{gradient} they do not, and that is the case this problem is made of.

A layer stack approximates a gradient by staircasing it.  That caps the
accuracy at second order in the step, however the layer response is computed,
because the error is then in the medium and not in the solver.  Integrating
\eqref{eq:impedance} through the true profile has no such cap.  Measured on a

\paragraph{What it does not buy.}  The economy above is in the depth direction

thesis's own criterion, weak superimposed heterogeneity, still governs whether
the series converges at all.

\section{The lateral coupling, carried inside the
march}\label{sec:imbedlateral}

\subsection{Where the thesis puts it, and where this puts it}

The thesis already solves the laterally varying problem, and it makes a
definite architectural choice.  Its eq.~\texttt{(CBPdef0)} assembles
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cbpdef}
  \bP_{r,s} \;=\; \bP^{z}_{r,s} + \bP^{x}_{r,s}
  \;=\; -i\,\Theta^{z}_{in}\,Q^{\partial}\,\Theta^{z}_{ou}
  \;+\; \mathrm{diag}\!\left[\bP^{x}_{r,s,1},\ \dots,\ \bP^{x}_{r,s,L}\right],
\end{equation}
acting on an $8LN_x$ vector --- eight augmented components, $L$ depth rows,
$N_x$ lateral sites.  Its contrast array \texttt{(dCBC)}, \texttt{(dCBCrho)} is
doubly block diagonal: over the $L$ rows, and within each row over the $N_x$
sites, each site carrying its own $8\times8$ $\dCeff(\mathbf{R}_j;k_y)$ weighted
by the cell area.  So the vertical operator is \emph{diagonal in lateral
wavenumber} --- Algorithm~5.3 is a mode-domain reflection/transmission recursion
at fixed $k_x$ --- and every lateral coupling lives in the site-diagonal
contrast together with the sideways sweeps $\bP^{x}$ of Algorithm~5.2, the two
being reconciled by an outer iteration on $(\bI - \bP\,\dCeff)$.

What follows does the opposite, and that is the only part of it that is new.
The lateral grid is carried \emph{inside} the marched variable: $\bY$ becomes a
matrix on $(\text{3 components})\times(N_x\ \text{lateral points})$ and the
lateral coupling is integrated along with depth, with no outer iteration for it
at all.  The geometry is the thesis's own $2\tfrac12$-D --- heterogeneity in
$(x,z)$, the third direction carried by the parameter $k_y$ --- so that the
thesis's solver remains available as an arbiter.

\subsection{The operator has to be derived, not read off}\label{sec:latop}

A laterally varying $\Aop$ cannot be transcribed from \texttt{(Akdef)}.  That
equation is a \emph{symbol} in $k_x$; it says nothing about where a spatially
varying modulus sits relative to a derivative, and the two orderings that
differ are indistinguishable in any laterally uniform medium.  Momentum balance
does say.  With $\zeta = 4\mu(\lambda+\mu)/\kappa$, $\chi = 2\mu\lambda/\kappa$
and $\gamma = \lambda/\kappa$, eliminating $T_{xx}$ and $T_{xy}$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:latrow}
  \partial_3 T_{xz} = -\rho\omega^{2}u_x
    - \partial_x\!\left[\zeta\,\partial_x u_x + \chi\,\partial_y u_y
      + \gamma\,T_{zz}\right]
    - \partial_y\!\left[\mu\left(\partial_x u_y + \partial_y u_x\right)\right],
\end{equation}
with the derivative \emph{outside} the modulus.  That ordering is forced by
$\partial_j T_{ij}$, not chosen, and it is what leaves the discretised operator
symplectic.  The lateral derivative is spectral on a periodic grid, hence
exactly antisymmetric, which turns the symplectic relation into an available
test rather than an approximation:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:latsymp}
  \Jsix\,\Aop(k_y) \;=\; \left[\Jsix\,\Aop(-k_y)\right]^{\mathsf{T}_x} .
\end{equation}
Here $\mathsf{T}_x$ denotes transposition in the \emph{lateral grid index
alone}, the six component indices being left as they are --- it is not the
$\tp$ used elsewhere in this note.  The parameter $k_y$ reverses with it
because in $2\tfrac12$-D it is a parameter and not a grid direction, and the
formal transpose of $\partial_x$ is $-\partial_x$ while a modulus is its own
transpose.  \eqref{eq:latsymp} reduces to \eqref{eq:recip} when the medium is
laterally uniform.  It
holds to $1.9\times10^{-16}$ for a laterally varying medium, and the plausible
wrong ordering --- $\mu\,\partial_x^2$ in place of
$\partial_x\mu\,\partial_x$ --- breaks it by $1.2\times10^{-1}$ while agreeing
with \eqref{eq:latrow} \emph{identically} when the medium does not vary.  No
laterally invariant test could have separated them.

Nothing is trusted on the strength of that alone.  Transformed to the lateral
wavenumber domain in a uniform medium the operator must reproduce
\texttt{(Akdef)} block by block, and does, to $10^{-15}$ at every grid
wavenumber, for two media and for $k_y = 0$ and $k_y\neq0$.

\subsection{What survives, measured}

The reduction is exact.  With the lateral contrast switched off but the depth
gradient left on, the coupled march must return a $\bY$ that is block diagonal
in $k_x$ whose $n$th block is the $3\times3$ march of \S\ref{sec:impmarch}.  It
is, to $3\times10^{-15}$, with no spurious coupling generated by the
discretisation ($6\times10^{-16}$).  Two structural identities come with it: at
$k_y=0$, $SH$ decouples from $P$--$SV$ exactly however the medium varies
laterally; and shifting the medium one grid point conjugates the marched
impedance by the shift, to $10^{-15}$, which is what catches an index slip
between the medium array and the derivative matrix.

This was found by the gate failing, and it matters for the economy claimed in
\S\ref{sec:whatimbedbuys}.  The right-hand side of \eqref{eq:impedance} has
decay rates set by the vertical wavenumbers, so the \emph{largest lateral}
wavenumber on the grid sets the largest rate, and explicit RK4 is stable only
while $2|k_x|_{\max}h$ is of order one --- measured, the march returns
\texttt{nan} at $2|k_x|_{\max}h = 1.31$ and is stable from $0.98$ down.  The
M\"obius step \eqref{eq:mobius}, being exact per sublayer, has no such limit and
is stable at every step tried.

The consequence is that the lateral mesh and the depth step are coupled:
refining laterally raises $|k_x|_{\max}$ and therefore forces a smaller depth
step for the explicit integrator.  That offsets part of the fourth-order economy
of \S\ref{sec:whatimbedbuys}, and how much of it is offset in any particular
calculation has not been measured.  An implicit or exponential integrator would
not pay it, and neither would the M\"obius step at the cost of returning to
second order.

\subsection{Where the cost actually is}\label{sec:latcost}

An earlier draft of this section asserted that
$\bR\,\bV_{du}\,\bR$ --- ``two dense products per step'' --- is the cost.
Building it says otherwise on both counts, and the reason is structural.
$\bA_{12}$ is the inverse vertical stiffness,
$\mathrm{diag}(1/\kappa,1/\mu,1/\mu)$: a pure multiplication operator carrying
no derivative, hence \emph{exactly} diagonal on the lateral grid.  So
$\bY\bA_{12}\bY$ needs one dense product, not two --- measured, $0.189$\,ms
naive against $0.093$\,ms exploiting the diagonal.  The other blocks are block
sparse at the component level, occupying $2$, $3$ and $2$ of nine $N_x\times
N_x$ sub-blocks, each occupied sub-block that carries a derivative being dense
laterally.  Counting one right-hand side in units of $N_x^{3}$:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrl@{}}
\toprule
term & flops & structure\\
\midrule
$\bY(\bA_{12}\bY)$ & $27$ & one dense $(3N_x)^3$ product; $\bA_{12}$ diagonal\\
$\bA_{22}\bY$      & $6$  & $2$ of $9$ sub-blocks occupied\\
$\bY\bA_{11}$      & $6$  & $2$ of $9$ sub-blocks occupied\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\captionof{table}{The quadratic term is $69\%$ of the step --- the dominant
single cost, but because the linear terms are block sparse, not because it is
two products.}
\end{center}

So the conclusion the earlier draft reached survives, with its stated reason
replaced.  The quadratic term is where a near-field preconditioner would act,
and that remains a claim about physics --- it is the term carrying the
back-scattering --- rather than about arithmetic.

\subsection{What this does not establish}

The march is built, reduces exactly, keeps its order and its symplectic
structure, and stays on the downgoing branch with $\|\bY\|$ constant to six
figures across a sixteen-fold change in step count.  What is \emph{not} measured
is any end-to-end advantage over the thesis's own architecture
\eqref{eq:cbpdef} on a problem either could solve.  That comparison needs a
laterally coupled arbiter, and the natural one --- the thesis's
$2\tfrac12$-D solver --- has not been run against it.  Until it has, the case
for putting the coupling inside the march rests on the structural results above,
on the exact-sphere verification of \S\ref{sec:threed}, and not on a measured
speed advantage against the thesis's own solver.

\section{Three-dimensional lateral structure, and the exact-sphere
verification}\label{sec:threed}

\subsection{Both lateral directions carried as grid directions}

Section~\ref{sec:latop} carries the lateral coupling inside the marched
variable along one direction, with the third held as the parameter $k_y$.  A
medium invariant in $y$ is then represented exactly and a medium that is not is
not represented at all: no single $k_y$ describes a sphere, however fine the
lateral grid.

Both directions become grid directions by the same construction.  The impedance
$\bY$ acts on $(3\ \text{components})\times(N_xN_y)$ lateral points, the lateral
index running $x$-slowest so that
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{D}_x = D_x\otimes\bI_{N_y},\qquad
  \mathbf{D}_y = \bI_{N_x}\otimes D_y ,
\end{equation}
each factor the spectral derivative of its own direction.  Every $i k_y$ of
\eqref{eq:latrow} becomes $\mathbf{D}_y$, and the modulus/derivative ordering that
momentum balance forces is the same ordering applied twice: the two horizontal
traction rows read
\begin{align}
  \partial_3 T_{xz} &= -\rho\omega^2 u_x
    - \partial_x\!\left[\zeta\,\partial_x u_x + \chi\,\partial_y u_y
      + \gamma\,T_{zz}\right]
    - \partial_y\!\left[\mu(\partial_x u_y + \partial_y u_x)\right], \\
  \partial_3 T_{yz} &= -\rho\omega^2 u_y
    - \partial_y\!\left[\chi\,\partial_x u_x + \zeta\,\partial_y u_y
      + \gamma\,T_{zz}\right]
    - \partial_x\!\left[\mu(\partial_x u_y + \partial_y u_x)\right],
\end{align}
with every derivative outside its modulus.  The constitutive rows keep the
modulus inside, because they are not divergences.

The symplectic statement becomes \emph{simpler}, not more complicated.  In
$2\tfrac12$-D it had to be written $\bJ_6\bA(k_y) = [\bJ_6\bA(-k_y)]^{\mathsf T}$
--- a transpose in the lateral index together with an explicit sign flip,
because $k_y$ is a parameter and transposition does nothing to a scalar.  Here
$\mathbf{D}_y$ is a real antisymmetric matrix, so it reverses its own sign under
transposition and the identity closes outright,
\begin{equation}
  \bJ_6\bA = (\bJ_6\bA)^{\mathsf T},
  \label{eq:symp3d}
\end{equation}
with no parameter to flip.  It holds at $8.7\times10^{-17}$, and the ordering
with the modulus outside every derivative pair --- indistinguishable in a
laterally uniform medium --- breaks it at $1.3\times10^{-1}$.

The operator reduces to objects established earlier: to $\bA$ of
\eqref{eq:quasiham} at each of the $N_xN_y$ wavenumber pairs in a uniform medium
($2.1\times10^{-15}$), to the $2\tfrac12$-D operator block by block in $k_y$
when the medium is invariant in $y$ ($3.6\times10^{-16}$), to the
$2\tfrac12$-D \emph{march} at every $k_y$ ($3.9\times10^{-15}$), and to the
scalar march of \S\ref{sec:impedance} at zero lateral contrast
($3.8\times10^{-15}$).  Shift covariance holds in each direction separately,
which is what distinguishes an index slip between the two Kronecker factors
from a consistent relabelling.

\subsection{The cross-section enters through its area, not its radius}

A sphere of radius $a$ centred at depth $z_c$ cuts the plane at depth $z$ in a
disc of radius $R(z)=\sqrt{a^2-\zeta^2}$, $\zeta = z-z_c$.  Painting that disc
onto the lateral grid cell by cell makes the marched coefficient piecewise
constant in depth: it changes in jumps as $R(z)$ crosses successive grid radii,
the integrator reproduces the staircase exactly, and the order of
\S\ref{sec:whatimbedbuys} is lost.

The disc has an exact two-dimensional transform,
\begin{equation}
  F(q,R) \;=\; \frac{2\pi R\,J_1(qR)}{q}
         \;=\; 2\pi R^2\,\frac{J_1(qR)}{qR},
  \qquad F(0)=\pi R^2 ,
  \label{eq:discff}
\end{equation}
and $J_1(x)/x$ is even and entire.  So $F$ is a power series in $(qR)^2$, hence
in $R^2 = a^2-\zeta^2$ --- a polynomial in depth.  \emph{The contrast taken in
wavenumber is therefore an analytic function of $z$ across the whole of the
sphere's bounding slab, poles included}, because the cross-sectional area enters
and not the radius, and the area vanishes linearly in $R^2$ where the radius has
a square root.  Marching over exactly $[z_c-a,\,z_c+a]$ places the only
non-smoothness --- the junction to the identically zero exterior --- at the
endpoints, never inside a step.

Measured on the sphere's own bounding slab, with the cross-section built from
\eqref{eq:discff} and sampled at the stage depths:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcccc@{}}
\toprule
 & \multicolumn{4}{c}{depth steps} \\
\cmidrule(l){2-5}
 & 32 & 64 & 128 & 256 \\
\midrule
exact transform   & $3.18\times 10^{-5}$ & $1.81\times 10^{-6}$ & $1.08\times 10^{-7}$ & $6.55\times 10^{-9}$ \\
observed order    & \multicolumn{4}{c}{$4.14\quad 4.07\quad 4.04$} \\[2pt]

Fourth order is attained on a sphere with a sharp boundary.  The control does
not merely converge more slowly: it has no order at all, because a staircase's
error depends on where the step boundaries happen to fall relative to the radius
crossings, so refinement moves the error rather than reducing it.

\subsection{The array relation}

The march is laterally periodic, so it solves an array of spheres, one per cell
of area $A=L_xL_y$.  At normal incidence every sphere sees the same incident
phase, so the array field is $\sum_{\mathbf{R}}\bu_{\rm sc}(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{R})$, and Poisson
summation gives
\begin{equation}
  \bu_{\rm array}
    = \frac{(2\pi)^2}{A}\sum_{\mathbf{g}}\hat{\bu}(\mathbf{g})\,
      e^{i\mathbf{g}\cdot\boldsymbol{\rho} + i k_z(\mathbf{g})|z|} ,
  \label{eq:poisson}
\end{equation}
so each diffraction order carries the isolated sphere's angular spectrum times
$(2\pi)^2/A$.  Referring the incident wave from the top of the bounding slab to
the sphere centre and the scattered order back again supplies the two phases,
and the reflection matrix extracted from $\bY$ at the top of the slab satisfies
\begin{equation}
  \bR[\mathbf{g}\!\leftarrow\!\mathbf{0}]
    = \frac{(2\pi)^2}{A}\,c_{\rm mode}(\mathbf{g})\,
      e^{i\left(k_P + k_z(\mathbf{g})\right)a} .
  \label{eq:arrayrel}
\end{equation}
The mode amplitudes $c_{\rm mode}$ are those of the elastic Mie solution,
assembled as plane-wave amplitudes rather than as a field: at one lateral
wavenumber $P$ and $S$ carry different $k_z$, so they cannot be summed into a
single vector before a depth is chosen.

Equation~\eqref{eq:arrayrel} is exact but for scattering \emph{between} array
elements, which is second order in the contrast where the signal is first.

\subsection{What the three refinement axes measure}

The comparison has three independent resolutions --- the depth step, the lateral
grid at fixed period, and the period at fixed pitch --- and they do not measure
the same kind of quantity.

Write $\varepsilon$ for the fractional perturbation of $\alpha$, $\beta$ and
$\rho$ inside the sphere.  Because the single-sphere signal is $O(\varepsilon)$
and the array coupling $O(\varepsilon^2)$, the \emph{relative} departure of the
march from \eqref{eq:arrayrel} must be linear in $\varepsilon$.  At
$k_Pa=1.44$, on an $8\times8$ grid of period $2.5$ diameters:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccccc@{}}
\toprule
$\varepsilon$ & $0.001$ & $0.003$ & $0.010$ & $0.030$ & $0.100$ \\
\midrule
relative departure & $0.00145$ & $0.00435$ & $0.01444$ & $0.04275$ & $0.13554$ \\
$/\varepsilon$     & $1.454$ & $1.451$ & $1.444$ & $1.425$ & $1.355$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
constant at $\simeq 1.45$ over two decades, the drift at the largest contrast
being the next order appearing.  The departure is therefore not an error of the
march but the coupling of the array, and $1.45\,\varepsilon$ measures its
strength at this spacing.  The marched amplitude exceeds the single-scattering
prediction in every row: at this spacing and frequency the neighbours enhance
the specular return.

With the coupling identified, the depth ladder converges \emph{to} it rather
than through it.  At $\varepsilon=10^{-3}$, where the coupling is

The lateral grid at fixed period moves by less than two per cent across

lateral discretisation error below $1.5\times10^{-3}$ without resolving it.  The
period at fixed pitch moves the floor itself --- $1.4543\times 10^{-3}$, $6.711\times 10^{-4}$,
$8.519\times 10^{-4}$ at $2.5$, $3.8$ and $5.0$ diameters --- falling as the lattice
opens and then rising, which is the oscillatory phase of the coupling and not
scatter: a $1/A$ falloff alone would fall monotonically.

\subsection{What this establishes}

The continuum impedance march, with the lateral coupling carried inside the
marched variable on a full three-dimensional lateral grid, reproduces the exact
elastic response of a sphere at fourth order in the depth step.  The sphere has
a sharp boundary, and the order survives it because the cross-section enters
through \eqref{eq:discff}.  The residual after the depth error is exhausted is
the array's own multiple scattering, measured at $1.45\,\varepsilon$ and linear
in the contrast over two decades.

\section{The algorithm}\label{sec:algorithm}

Everything above is stated as identities and measurements; what follows collects
it as a procedure, in the form Chapter~5 of the thesis uses for
Algorithms~5.1--5.3.  One algorithm covers every case treated here.  With
$N_x=N_y=1$ and the lateral derivatives replaced by $ik_x$, $ik_y$ it is the
laterally invariant march of \S\ref{sec:impedance}; with $N_y=1$ and
$\mathbf{D}_y = ik_y\bI$ it is the $2\tfrac12$-D march of \S\ref{sec:latop};
with both directions gridded it is the three-dimensional march of
\S\ref{sec:threed}.  Nothing in the body of the march distinguishes them, which
is the point of carrying the coupling inside the marched variable.

Three things in it are not free choices, and each is marked where it occurs:
the modulus/derivative ordering in $\bA_{21}$ and the lower rows of $\bA_{22}$,
which momentum balance forces; the sampling of $\bA$ at the \emph{stage} depths
rather than once per sublayer, without which the order falls to two; and the
starting impedance, which must solve the algebraic Riccati equation of the
half-space rather than be read off $\mathbf{D}_z$ alone.

\vspace{3mm}
\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\textbf{Algorithm}~\textbf{1} {\sc A continuum impedance march for the
reflection of a laterally varying slab}\newline
\vspace{2mm}

\begin{itemize}
\item given a slab $0\le z\le H$ over a laterally uniform half-space, a lateral
grid of $N=N_xN_y$ points with spectral derivatives $\mathbf{D}_x$,
$\mathbf{D}_y$, and a depth partition $0=z_0<z_1<\cdots<z_M=H$ numbered
\emph{downward} as in Chapter~5, with sublayer thicknesses
$h_m=z_m-z_{m-1}>0$, compute the impedance $\bY$ at the top of the slab and the
reflection matrix $\bR$

\begin{itemize}
\item (*{\em the operator at one depth --- every modulus is a multiplication
operator on the lateral grid\/}*)
\end{itemize}

\item \textbf{Procedure} $\bA(z)$:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\kappa=\lambda+2\mu$, \quad $\gamma=\lambda/\kappa$, \quad
      $\zeta = 4\mu(\lambda+\mu)/\kappa$, \quad $\chi = 2\mu\lambda/\kappa$,
      \quad all evaluated from $(\alpha,\beta,\rho)$ at depth $z$
\item $\bA_{11}=\begin{bmatrix}
        0 & -\gamma\mathbf{D}_x & -\gamma\mathbf{D}_y\\
        -\mathbf{D}_x & 0 & 0\\ -\mathbf{D}_y & 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}$,
      \qquad
      $\bA_{12}=\mathrm{diag}\!\left(\kappa^{-1},\,\mu^{-1},\,\mu^{-1}\right)$
\item $\bA_{21}=-\begin{bmatrix}
        \rho\omega^{2} & 0 & 0\\
        0 & \rho\omega^{2}+\mathcal{X} & \mathcal{C}\\
        0 & \mathcal{C}^{\ast} & \rho\omega^{2}+\mathcal{Y}\end{bmatrix}$,
      \qquad
      $\bA_{22}=-\begin{bmatrix}
        0 & \mathbf{D}_x & \mathbf{D}_y\\
        \mathbf{D}_x\gamma & 0 & 0\\
        \mathbf{D}_y\gamma & 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}$
\item with, \textbf{the derivative outside the modulus},
      \[
        \begin{aligned}
          \mathcal{X} &= \mathbf{D}_x\zeta\mathbf{D}_x
                       + \mathbf{D}_y\mu\mathbf{D}_y, &\qquad
          \mathcal{C} &= \mathbf{D}_x\chi\mathbf{D}_y
                       + \mathbf{D}_y\mu\mathbf{D}_x, \\
          \mathcal{Y} &= \mathbf{D}_y\zeta\mathbf{D}_y
                       + \mathbf{D}_x\mu\mathbf{D}_x, &
          \mathcal{C}^{\ast} &= \mathbf{D}_y\chi\mathbf{D}_x
                       + \mathbf{D}_x\mu\mathbf{D}_y
        \end{aligned}
      \]
\item (*{\em $\zeta\mathbf{D}_x\mathbf{D}_x$ in place of
      $\mathbf{D}_x\zeta\mathbf{D}_x$ agrees exactly in a laterally uniform
      medium and breaks \eqref{eq:symp3d}\/}*)
\item \textbf{Return} $(\bA_{11},\bA_{12},\bA_{21},\bA_{22})$
\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}
\item (*{\em bottom of the slab, $z_M=H$: the half-space impedance, one
$3\times3$ block
per lateral wavenumber\/}*)
\end{itemize}

\item \textbf{For} each lateral wavenumber $(k_x,k_y)$ of the grid
\begin{itemize}
\item $\bY^{(0)} = \mathbf{T}_d\mathbf{U}_d^{-1}$ from the three
      \emph{downgoing} columns of $\mathbf{D}_z$
\item \textbf{Repeat} until the residual of \eqref{eq:impedance} stops falling
\begin{itemize}
\item $\mathbf{F} = \bA_{21}+\bA_{22}\bY-\bY\bA_{11}-\bY\bA_{12}\bY$
\item solve
      $(\bA_{22}-\bY\bA_{12})\,\mathbf{E}-\mathbf{E}\,(\bA_{11}+\bA_{12}\bY)
       = -\mathbf{F}$ \quad for $\mathbf{E}$
\item $\bY \leftarrow \bY+\mathbf{E}$
\end{itemize}
\item (*{\em $\mathbf{D}_z$ names the branch; $\bA$ supplies the precision\/}*)
\end{itemize}
\item assemble the blocks block-diagonally and transform to the lateral grid
\end{itemize}
\end{minipage}

\vspace{4mm}
\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\textbf{Algorithm}~\textbf{1} {\em continued\/}\newline
\vspace{2mm}

\begin{itemize}
\item (*{\em upsweep\/}*)

\item \textbf{For} $m=M,\cdots,1$ \quad --- $\bY$ enters each step at $z_m$ and
      leaves it at $z_{m-1}$, a distance $h_m$ \emph{upward}
\begin{itemize}
\item $\mathbf{K}_1 = \mathcal{F}\!\left(\bY,\;\bA(z_{m})\right)$
\item $\mathbf{K}_2 = \mathcal{F}\!\left(\bY-\tfrac{h_m}{2}\mathbf{K}_1,\;
        \bA(z_{m}-\tfrac{h_m}{2})\right)$
\item $\mathbf{K}_3 = \mathcal{F}\!\left(\bY-\tfrac{h_m}{2}\mathbf{K}_2,\;
        \bA(z_{m}-\tfrac{h_m}{2})\right)$
\item $\mathbf{K}_4 = \mathcal{F}\!\left(\bY-h_m\mathbf{K}_3,\;
        \bA(z_{m-1})\right)$
\item $\bY \leftarrow \bY
        - \tfrac{h_m}{6}\left(\mathbf{K}_1+2\mathbf{K}_2+2\mathbf{K}_3
        +\mathbf{K}_4\right)$
\item (*{\em the minus signs are the upsweep: $h_m>0$ is a thickness, and the
      march runs against increasing $z$\/}*)
\item (*{\em $\bA$ is rebuilt at each stage depth.  Freezing it per sublayer
      integrates a staircase exactly and returns second order\/}*)
\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}
\item (*{\em or, unconditionally stable and second order, with $\bA$ frozen at
      the midpoint --- one solve, no exponential\/}*)
\end{itemize}
\item $\bA_{\mathrm{mid}}=\bA\!\left(z_m-\tfrac{h_m}{2}\right)$, \quad
      solve
      $\left(\bI+\tfrac{h_m}{2}\bA_{\mathrm{mid}}\right)\mathbf{W}
       = \left(\bI-\tfrac{h_m}{2}\bA_{\mathrm{mid}}\right)
         \begin{bmatrix}\bI\\ \bY\end{bmatrix}$
      \quad for $\mathbf{W}$
\item $\bY \leftarrow \mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{lower}}\,
        \mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{upper}}^{-1}$
\item (*{\em no matrix exponential is formed.  The signs are the upsweep: the
      step advances from $z_m$ to $z_{m-1}$, and follows from $\bq'=\bA\bq$\/}*)

\begin{itemize}
\item (*{\em the reflection matrix at the top\/}*)
\end{itemize}

\item transform $\bY$ to the lateral wavenumber domain, giving $\hat{\bY}$
\item build $\mathbf{U}_d,\mathbf{T}_d,\mathbf{U}_u,\mathbf{T}_u$
      block-diagonally, each column a unit-displacement mode of the medium
      above
\item $\bR = \left(\mathbf{T}_u-\hat{\bY}\mathbf{U}_u\right)^{-1}
             \left(\hat{\bY}\mathbf{U}_d-\mathbf{T}_d\right)$
\end{itemize}
\end{minipage}

\vspace{3mm}
\noindent where
$\mathcal{F}(\bY,\bA)=\bA_{21}+\bA_{22}\bY-\bY\bA_{11}-\bY\bA_{12}\bY$ is the
right-hand side of \eqref{eq:impedance}.

\paragraph{Which integrator, and when.}  The two marching steps are
alternatives, not successive stages.  The explicit one attains fourth order but
is conditionally stable: the right-hand side of \eqref{eq:impedance} carries
decay rates set by the largest vertical wavenumber, so the largest lateral
wavenumber on the grid sets the limit, and the step becomes unstable once
$2\,|k|_{\max}h \gtrsim 1.3$.  \emph{Refining the lateral grid therefore
forces a smaller depth step}, which offsets part of the advantage measured in
\S\ref{sec:whatimbedbuys} by an amount that has not been quantified.  The
stable step freezes the medium at the midpoint and is second order --- confirmed,
with errors falling by $4.00$, $4.00$, $4.00$ over a fourfold refinement --- and
it is one solve of the $6N$ system rather than an exponential of it.  The next
paragraph says why, because the imbedding literature's step is the exponential
and the difference is worth stating.

\paragraph{Why the stable step is a solve and not an exponential.}  The
M\"obius step of the imbedding literature freezes the medium in each sublayer
and propagates with $\exp(\bA h)$, which is exact for that frozen medium.  It is
also an exponential of the \emph{full} $6N\times6N$ operator, and the impedance
form exists precisely to avoid handling that object.  Measured against one
explicit step, at $h=-H/32$:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccccc@{}}
\toprule
grid & $3N$ & explicit step & $\exp(\bA h)$ & ratio & one solve \\
\midrule
$6\times6$   & $108$ & $3.48$\,ms & $10.45$\,ms & $3.0$ & $2.51$\,ms \\
$8\times8$   & $192$ & $9.46$\,ms & $33.43$\,ms & $3.5$ & $7.93$\,ms \\
$10\times10$ & $300$ & $25.13$\,ms & $71.06$\,ms & $2.8$ & $20.93$\,ms \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
about three times the explicit step, the ratio flat in $N$ because both scale as
$N^{3}$: $(6N)^{3}=8(3N)^{3}$ against four right-hand sides of roughly four
$(3N)^{3}$ products each.

\emph{The exactness buys nothing.}  Freezing the medium at the midpoint already
makes the step second order, so reproducing the frozen sublayer exactly is
accuracy spent where the method cannot use it.  Replacing the exponential by its
$(1,1)$ Pad\'e,
$\mathbf{M}=(\bI-\tfrac{h}{2}\bA)^{-1}(\bI+\tfrac{h}{2}\bA)$, keeps the order
and the $A$-stability and costs one solve:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcccc@{}}
\toprule
depth steps & $4$ & $8$ & $16$ & $32$ \\
\midrule
$2|k|_{\max}h$ & $1.26$ & $0.63$ & $0.31$ & $0.16$ \\
$\exp(\bA h)$ & $2.80\times10^{-2}$ & $6.99\times10^{-3}$
              & $1.74\times10^{-3}$ & $4.30\times10^{-4}$ \\
one solve     & $4.17\times10^{-2}$ & $1.08\times10^{-2}$
              & $2.72\times10^{-3}$ & $6.81\times10^{-4}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
observed orders $2.00,2.00,2.02$ against $1.95,1.99,2.00$, and a constant factor
of about $1.6$ in the error for a factor of $3.3$ in cost.  It holds where the
explicit step does not: at $2|k|_{\max}h = 2.51$ the explicit march returns
$4.33$ --- it has diverged --- and the solve returns $1.41\times10^{-1}$.  That
is what the stable branch is for, and it is why the algorithm above forms no
exponential.

Two remarks, one of them a dead end worth recording.  The exponential is never
needed even when it is used, because the update takes only
$\mathbf{M}_{21}+\mathbf{M}_{22}\bY$ and $\mathbf{M}_{11}+\mathbf{M}_{12}\bY$,
the two halves of $\mathbf{M}[\bI;\bY]$; but computing that \emph{action} by a
Krylov method, while it agrees to $2.5\times10^{-15}$, runs about twice as
slowly as forming the exponential outright, because at these dense sizes
scaling-and-squaring is the faster algorithm.  And the Pad\'e is not the
exponential: for $|\lambda h|$ large it tends to $-\bI$ where the exponential
tends to $\mathbf{0}$, so a strongly evanescent sublayer mode is held rather
than decayed.  That did not bite over the range above, where the largest lateral
wavenumber on the grid was carried at $2|k|_{\max}h$ up to $2.51$; a slab stiff
enough to expose it would want the exponential back.

\paragraph{Cost.}  $\bA_{12}$ is diagonal --- it carries no derivative --- so
the quadratic term of $\mathcal{F}$ is a single dense product of
$3N\times3N$ matrices.  It nevertheless dominates, because the linear terms are
block sparse: two of the nine $N\times N$ sub-blocks of $\bA_{11}$ and
$\bA_{22}$ are non-zero.  Per right-hand side, in units of $N^3$: the quadratic
term $27$, $\bA_{22}\bY$ and $\bY\bA_{11}$ six apiece, so the quadratic term is
$69\%$ of the work and the preconditioner, when one is built, belongs there.

\subsection{The plane-wave R/T arrays of a
sphere}\label{sec:algorithm-rt}

Algorithm~1 stops at $\bR$, which is all one sweep can give.  The procedure
below is the whole response --- both matrices, every incident and outgoing
channel --- and it is stated separately because three of its steps are not
refinements of Algorithm~1 but additions to it: the second sweep, the impedance
\emph{history} that sweep consumes, and the cross-section that puts a sphere on
a periodic grid without a staircase.

Four things in it are not free choices.  The cross-section enters through its
\emph{area} and not its radius, so the disc is band-limited rather than
voxelised (\S\ref{sec:threed}).  The march domain is exactly $[-a,+a]$ about the
centre, so the only non-smoothness in the coefficient --- the junction to the
homogeneous exterior --- sits at the endpoints and never inside a step.  The
upward sweep is run at \emph{twice} the depth resolution of the downward one and
every value kept, so that the downward Runge--Kutta finds $\bY$ already
evaluated at each of its stage depths and no interpolation enters; without that
the second sweep is second order and the first is wasted.  And the azimuthal
convention on the array's specular order is shared between the mode matrices and
the multipole spectrum, because on axis neither is defined alone.

\vspace{3mm}
\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\textbf{Algorithm}~\textbf{2} {\sc The plane-wave reflection and transmission
arrays of a sphere}\newline
\vspace{2mm}

\begin{itemize}
\item given a sphere of radius $a$ and contrast $(\Delta\lambda,\Delta\mu,
\Delta\rho)$ centred in a cell of area $A=L_xL_y$ repeated periodically, a
lateral grid of $N=N_xN_y$ points, and $M$ depth steps, compute $\bR$ and $\bT$
for every incident and outgoing plane-wave channel

\begin{itemize}
\item (*{\em the medium at one depth: a disc of the right AREA, band-limited to
the grid\/}*)
\end{itemize}

\item \textbf{Procedure} $\mathrm{slice}(\zeta)$:
\begin{itemize}
\item $R^{2} = a^{2}-\zeta^{2}$ \quad (and the slice is the exterior medium
      where $R^{2}\le0$)
\item $\widehat{\Pi}(\mathbf{q}) = 2\pi R^{2}\,
      \dfrac{J_1(|\mathbf{q}|R)}{|\mathbf{q}|R}$ \quad on the grid's lateral
      wavenumbers, with the value $\pi R^{2}$ at $\mathbf{q}=0$
\item (*{\em $J_1(x)/x$ is even and entire, so $\widehat{\Pi}$ is analytic in
      $R^{2}=a^{2}-\zeta^{2}$ and therefore smooth across the pole.  A voxelised
      disc is not, and the fourth order does not survive it\/}*)
\item $\Pi(\mathbf{x})$ = the inverse lateral transform of $\widehat{\Pi}/A$, centred on the
      cell by the shift phase
\item \textbf{Return} $(\lambda,\mu,\rho)(\mathbf{x}) =
      (\lambda_0,\mu_0,\rho_0) + \Pi(\mathbf{x})\,
      (\Delta\lambda,\Delta\mu,\Delta\rho)$
\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}
\item (*{\em sweep one: upward, at DOUBLE resolution, keeping every value\/}*)
\end{itemize}

\item partition $[-a,+a]$ into $2M$ steps $\zeta_0=-a<\cdots<\zeta_{2M}=+a$,
      where $\zeta=z-a$ is measured from the sphere's centre and increases
      \emph{downward}: $\zeta_{2M}$ is the base and $\zeta_0$ the top
\item $\bY \leftarrow$ the half-space impedance of the exterior medium, by the
      Newton loop of Algorithm~1
\item store $\mathbf{Y}_{2M}\leftarrow\bY$
\item \textbf{For} $j=2M,\cdots,1$: advance $\bY$ from $\zeta_j$ to
      $\zeta_{j-1}$ by one step of Algorithm~1, then store
      $\mathbf{Y}_{j-1}\leftarrow\bY$
\item (*{\em the stored values at even $j$ are the downward sweep's step edges
      and those at odd $j$ its midpoints --- exactly its stage depths\/}*)
\end{itemize}
\end{minipage}

\vspace{4mm}
\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\textbf{Algorithm}~\textbf{2} {\em continued\/}\newline
\vspace{2mm}

\begin{itemize}
\item (*{\em the mode matrices, and the one convention that is shared\/}*)

\item \textbf{For} each lateral wavenumber $\mathbf{q}=(k_x,k_y)$ of the grid,
build the $3\times3$ blocks $\bU_d,\mathbf{T}_d,\bU_u,\mathbf{T}_u$ of the
exterior medium, columns ordered $P,SV,SH$ and each of unit displacement
\item on the specular order $\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{0}$ take the azimuth
      $\psi=0$, so that $\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{SV}=\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ and
      $\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{SH}=\hat{\mathbf{y}}$
\item (*{\em the azimuth has no limit there, but the field does: the
      $\psi$-dependence of these polarisations cancels against that of the
      $m=\pm1$ multipole spectrum they are compared with, so the SAME branch
      must be taken in both.  Taking $0/0=0$ instead zeroes the entire specular
      column\/}*)

\begin{itemize}
\item (*{\em reflection, from the top of the first sweep alone\/}*)
\end{itemize}

\item $\hat{\bY}\leftarrow$ $\bY$ transformed to the lateral wavenumber domain
\item $\bR = \left(\mathbf{T}_u-\hat{\bY}\bU_u\right)^{-1}
             \left(\hat{\bY}\bU_d-\mathbf{T}_d\right)$

\begin{itemize}
\item (*{\em sweep two: downward, WITH the energy flow, while the Riccati ran
against it\/}*)
\end{itemize}

\item $\bu \leftarrow \bU_d + \bU_u\bR$ \quad --- the total displacement at the
      top for unit incidence in each channel, transformed to the lateral grid
\item \textbf{For} $m=1,\cdots,M$ \quad --- with $j=2(m-1)$ and step
      $h=\zeta_{j+2}-\zeta_{j}>0$
\begin{itemize}
\item $\mathbf{K}_1 = \mathcal{O}_{j}\,\bu$, \quad
      $\mathbf{K}_2 = \mathcal{O}_{j+1}\!\left(\bu+\tfrac{h}{2}\mathbf{K}_1\right)$
\item $\mathbf{K}_3 = \mathcal{O}_{j+1}\!\left(\bu+\tfrac{h}{2}\mathbf{K}_2\right)$,
      \quad
      $\mathbf{K}_4 = \mathcal{O}_{j+2}\!\left(\bu+h\mathbf{K}_3\right)$
\item $\bu \leftarrow \bu + \tfrac{h}{6}\left(\mathbf{K}_1+2\mathbf{K}_2
      +2\mathbf{K}_3+\mathbf{K}_4\right)$
\end{itemize}
\item (*{\em the signs are the downsweep, and it is the stable direction here
      for the same reason the upsweep is stable for $\bY$\/}*)

\begin{itemize}
\item (*{\em transmission: below the slab the field is purely downgoing\/}*)
\end{itemize}

\item $\bT = \bU_d^{-1}\,\hat{\bu}$, \quad with $\hat{\bu}$ the field at
      $\zeta_{2M}$ returned to the wavenumber domain
\end{itemize}
\end{minipage}

\vspace{3mm}
\noindent where
$\mathcal{O}_j = \bA_{11}(\zeta_j)+\bA_{12}(\zeta_j)\,\mathbf{Y}_j$ is the
one-way operator of \eqref{eq:oneway}, built from the stored impedance at that
depth and never re-marched.

\paragraph{What it costs, against Algorithm~1.}  Measured at $N=36$, $M=16$:
$2.49\times$ the reflection alone.  Most of that is not the second sweep.  The
\emph{upward} sweep is what doubles --- it runs at $2M$ steps so that the
downward one finds $\bY$ at its stage depths --- and the downward sweep itself
is $M$ steps of four matrix--vector products against $\mathcal{O}$, cheaper per
step than the Riccati by the whole quadratic term.  So the price of transmission
is paid mostly in the sweep one already had.

\emph{An earlier draft of this paragraph said $1.5\times$, reasoning from the
cheapness of the second sweep and forgetting that the first one had been
doubled.}  The memory cost is the history, $2M+1$ matrices of $3N\times3N$,
which is the only new storage in the procedure and the reason the doubling is
stated as part of the algorithm rather than left to the caller.

\paragraph{Checking it without an arbiter.}  Two conditions are available before
any comparison with Mie, and both are stated in \S\ref{sec:rt}.  At zero
contrast every entry is known in closed form, $\bR=\mathbf{0}$ and
$\bT=\mathrm{diag}\,e^{ik_{z,c}\cdot2a}$, which tests the sweeps and the mode
matrices together.  At finite contrast, rescaling to the flux basis by
$w_c(\mathbf{q})=\sqrt{c^{2}k_{z,c}/\omega}$ and restricting to propagating
channels, $\bR^{\dagger}\bR+\bT^{\dagger}\bT=\bI$ --- which scores every entry
of both matrices at once, and needs nothing external.

\paragraph{Against the isolated sphere.}  To compare with Mie the array must be
undone.  One sphere per cell of area $A$, all seeing the same incident phase at
normal incidence, gives by Poisson summation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:poisson-rt}
  \bR[\mathbf{G}\!\leftarrow\!\Gamma]
  = \frac{(2\pi)^2}{A}\,c_{\mathrm{mode}}(\mathbf{G})\,
    e^{i(k_{\mathrm{inc}}+k_z(\mathbf{G}))a},
\end{equation}
with the same expression for $\bT$ plus the unscattered wave
$e^{2ik_{\mathrm{inc}}a}$ on the specular entry of the incident channel.  The
two phases are the incident wave referred from the top of the slab to the
sphere's centre, where Mie normalises it, and the scattered order referred back
from the centre to the plane.  The spectrum $c_{\mathrm{mode}}$ is the $m=0$ one
for $P$ incidence and the $m=\pm1$ one for $S$; \eqref{eq:poisson-rt} is exact
but for scattering \emph{between} spheres, which is what growing $A$ removes and
what the residual of \S\ref{sec:threed} measures at $1.45\,\varepsilon$.

\section{What is not established}\label{sec:open}

In keeping with the project's standard that an unvalidated formula be labelled
as such, the following are \emph{open}:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The \emph{iterative} multiple-scattering solver has never been
      compared, because it has no problem to be compared on.}
      \S\ref{sec:vsfoldy} scores the march against the voxel route, but that
      route solves a sphere by a \emph{direct} dense solve of the $9N_c$
      system.  The GMRES solver in this project works on a uniform periodic
      slab, whose geometry is a fully occupied lattice with no per-cell
      occupancy and which therefore cannot hold a sphere --- so the one
      configuration with an exact external arbiter is closed to it.  What is
      needed is a Bloch-periodic Foldy--Lax over a \emph{voxelised sphere},
      which would put the iterative solver on the same problem as the march
      and, unlike the present comparison, on the same periodic array.  Until
      then nothing here says anything about iterative cost, and in particular
      the iteration count's growth with contrast --- the axis on which the
      sweep architecture is known to do nothing --- remains unmeasured against
      the march, which has no iteration count at all.

\item \textbf{The $T$-matrix uses the collocation propagator, not the
      consistent one.}  $\mathbf{T}$ is assembled in \S\ref{sec:tmatrix} and
      calibrated exactly, but $\mathcal{G}$ there is the \emph{single} cube
      moment while the $\Jsix$ pairing makes the scheme Galerkin.  This is now
      measured rather than suspected: \eqref{eq:gapfactor} separates the gap
      into a geometric factor $\int_V 1/r \big/ D_0 = 1.2644$ and a factor
      $i\omega$, and the double moment $D_0$ that the consistent pairing needs is
      extracted in \S\ref{sec:schwinger}.  The $i\omega$ in
      \eqref{eq:gapfactor} is \emph{not} open --- see \S\ref{sec:units} --- so
      what remains is the \textbf{geometric} factor alone.  Until that is
      resolved, everything in \S\ref{sec:tmatrix} is the first-order coupling
      seen through a collocation propagator.
\item \textbf{The external arbiter reaches the operator, not the cube.}
      \S\ref{sec:layer} closes this item for $\Aop$, $\boldsymbol\Gamma$, their
      composition and the resummation: a layer in a whole space reproduces an
      independently validated reflectivity recursion across contrast and angle.
      But a laterally invariant problem does not touch the augmentation, the
      form factors or the moments, so the quantities in \S\ref{sec:whatitbuys}
      and \S\ref{sec:tmatrix} still compare two \emph{formulations} with each
      other rather than either against truth.  The $\Oh$ split remains the only
      partial exception there, and it is one-sided: it lower-bounds the
      first-order error at $\approx4.2\times10^{-4}$ and bounds the ordinary
      route not at all.  \S\ref{sec:whichbetter} then answers the accuracy
      question for the cube by the one geometry in which both formulations
      apply and an external reference exists --- a space-filling patch --- and
      the first-order $T$ wins there by a margin that grows with contrast.  What
      remains genuinely open is the \emph{scope} of that: one frequency, normal
      incidence, an unconverged lattice, and no measurement at all away from
      space-filling.
\item \textbf{Whether the resummation improves accuracy is unknown.}  It is
      exact as a local inversion; that is not the same claim.
\item \textbf{$\Oh$ invariance was tested on a subgroup of order $3$}, not the
      full group.
\item \textbf{The derivative count for a collocation scheme differs from the
      Galerkin one.}  In the Galerkin pairing the transferred derivative lands
      on polynomials, so the third-derivative moment cannot arise; under
      collocation it transfers onto $\boldsymbol\Gamma$ and can.  Do not quote
      the Galerkin count for a collocation build.  \eqref{eq:ikrule} is how that
      transfer is carried out in practice, and it is verified; what is not yet
      done is the count itself.
\item \textbf{The derivative channels are verified only by symmetry.}  That is a
      strong test --- ten orders of separation from its control, and the
      derivative terms carry $80\%$ of the matrix --- but symmetry is a
      \emph{structural} property.  A bookkeeping error that is symmetric would
      pass it.  The value of those channels has not been compared with an
      independent arbiter, which would need the Galerkin double-volume moments of
      $\partial_i\partial_j G_{kl}$; those follow from the same autocorrelation
      and Duffy construction as $D_0$, with $\partial_i\partial_j$ moved onto the
      weight, but they have not been computed.
\item \textbf{The tetragonal $T_0$ is measured but not shipped.}
      \S\ref{sec:tetragonal} builds it and shows it collects the $x_3$ benefit,
      $3.2\times$ in the channel that probes it.  But it is built in the gate,
      not in the package: \texttt{effective\_stiffness\_voigt} still takes one
      shear parameter and writes it into all three slots.  Widening it is a small
      backwards-compatible change, and the right moment for it is when the
      first-order route is wired into the package proper rather than now, when
      nothing upstream in the package produces two shear amplitudes.
\item \textbf{The laterally coupled march has no external arbiter, and no
      measured advantage.}  \S\ref{sec:imbedlateral} establishes that the
      construction is sound --- it reduces exactly to the validated
      $3\times3$ march, keeps fourth order, keeps the symplectic structure
      \eqref{eq:latsymp}, and agrees with an independent algorithm on the same
      operator.  Every one of those is internal.  Nothing yet compares it with
      an object built outside this development, and in particular nothing
      compares it with the thesis's own architecture \eqref{eq:cbpdef}, which
      solves the same problem by keeping the vertical operator diagonal in
      $k_x$.  The natural arbiter is the thesis's $2\tfrac12$-D solver; until it
      has been run, ``carrying the coupling inside the march'' is a structural
      result and not a demonstrated improvement.  The cost trade is likewise
      only half measured: the quadratic term is $69\%$ of a step, but the
      explicit integrator's step limit tightens as the lateral mesh is refined,
      and how much of the fourth-order economy that consumes has not been
      quantified.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Against multiple scattering, on the one validated
problem}\label{sec:vsfoldy}

The sphere is the only problem in this note with an exact external solution and
a laterally varying medium at once, so it is the only place the imbedding route
can be scored against the alternative the rest of this project is built on: a
body voxelised into cubes, each carrying an analytic $T$-matrix, coupled and
solved.  Both compute the same physical quantity --- the backscattered $P$
amplitude of a sphere at $k_Sa=2.4$, contrast $\varepsilon=0.1$ --- and both are
scored against exact Mie.

\paragraph{Two corrections to the framing, before the numbers.}  The voxel route
\emph{as applied to a sphere} is not iterative: it ends in a direct dense solve
of the $9N_c$ system.  The iterative (GMRES) multiple-scattering solver in this
project works on a uniform periodic slab whose geometry is a fully occupied
lattice with no per-cell occupancy, so it cannot hold a sphere --- and therefore
has no externally validated problem to be compared on at all.  Closing that gap
needs a Bloch-periodic Foldy--Lax over a voxelised sphere, which is not built.

And the arbiter is \emph{not neutral}.  Exact Mie is the isolated sphere.  The
voxel route also solves the isolated sphere, so it is charged only for its own
discretisation.  The march solves an \emph{array}, and most of what is charged
against it is the inter-sphere coupling of \S\ref{sec:threed} --- real physics
it includes correctly and the arbiter does not contain.  The comparison is
therefore biased \emph{against} the march.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrr@{}}
\toprule
& unknowns & error vs Mie & time (s) \\
\midrule
voxel Foldy--Lax, $n_{\rm sub}=6$ & $1224$ & $0.379$  & $1.26$\\
impedance march, $N=81$, $L=900$  & $\mathbf{243}$  & $\mathbf{0.0112}$ & $1.16$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\captionof{table}{At matched cost: $34\times$ the accuracy on a fifth of the

scaling.}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Neither error is a discretisation error, and neither ladder
converges.}  This is the part worth stating carefully, because the obvious
reading of the table --- that one method is converging faster --- is wrong.
Refining either knob does \emph{not} reduce either error monotonically:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrr@{}}
\toprule
voxel route, $n_{\rm sub}$ & $3$ & $4$ & $6$ & $8$\\
error & $0.502$ & $\mathbf{0.205}$ & $0.379$ & $0.282$\\
\midrule
march, period (diameters) & $2.5$ & $3.8$ & $5.0$ & $6.7$\\
error & $0.135$ & $\mathbf{0.011}$ & $0.082$ & $0.047$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\captionof{table}{Both are non-monotone, for different reasons.  The voxel
route's error is the \emph{staircase}, and a cubic lattice's coverage of a
sphere is not monotone in $n_{\rm sub}$ --- the voxel volume is $26\%$, $4.7\%$,
$17\%$ and $4.3\%$ wrong at these four.  The march's is \emph{array coupling},
which is a wave interference and so oscillates with spacing rather than
decaying.  Quoting a convergence rate for either on this problem would be
wrong.}
\end{center}

\noindent
The difference that survives is in the floors, not the rates.  Forty times the
cells buys the voxel route nothing: its best is $0.205$ and refinement does not
go below it, because what fails is the representation of a curved boundary at a
wavelength comparable with the radius --- the same conclusion \S\ref{sec:threed}
reached from the other direction, and the reason the cross-section here is
band-limited rather than voxelised.  The march's floor is not a floor of the
method at all: it is the array, and it moves when the array does.

\paragraph{What this does and does not settle.}  It settles that on this
problem, at this frequency and contrast, the imbedding route is the more
accurate of the two per unknown by a wide margin, while being scored against an
arbiter that does not contain the physics it is charged for.  It does not settle
anything about the \emph{iterative} solver, which was never in the comparison.
It also does not settle the asymptotic cost of either: four points at these
sizes are descriptive, and the dense products are not in their cubic regime.
The comparison that would settle the remaining question --- iterative multiple
scattering against imbedding, on a problem with an external arbiter --- needs
the periodic voxel sphere that \S\ref{sec:open} now lists.

\section{Where the route belongs}

The $x_3$ preference of \S\ref{sec:oh} is an artefact only because the problem
posed there --- an isotropic cube in an isotropic whole space --- has no
preferred direction.  A layered background does.  There the formulation's
directional structure matches the physics rather than contradicting it, the
$\Oh$ argument does not apply, and the propagator is already available and
already bridged to $\Aop$ by \eqref{eq:bridge}.

That was written as an expectation and is now a measurement.  In the stratified
geometry of \S\ref{sec:whichbetter}, the $x_3$-distinguished shear amplitude is
the one the physics wants, by a factor of $2.6$ in the channel that probes it
(\S\ref{sec:whichchannel}).  The same feature that is demonstrably wrong in a
whole space --- $\ge4.2\times10^{-4}$ of error, by \S\ref{sec:oh} --- is
demonstrably right once there is a stack of interfaces for it to be right about.
Both statements are measured, and they are not in tension: they are about
different problems.

That is also the case in which the ordinary route has no competitor: the cube
moment method rests on volume moments of the whole-space Green's tensor, and in
a stratified background that object does not exist.  The first-order form
supplies what the moment method cannot.

\section{Against the literature}\label{sec:priorart}

The construction marched in \S\ref{sec:impedance} and extended in
\S\ref{sec:latop} and \S\ref{sec:threed} is not new.  It is the impedance
operator approach of Haines, Hulme \& Yu \cite{haines2004}, and the symmetry relied on
throughout as a structural check is the impedance-operator symmetry of Thomson
\cite{thomson2015a,thomson2015b}.  Neither is cited in the thesis or anywhere in the codebase this
document accompanies, which is how the omission survived: the survey that
precedes work here covers five places, and all five are local.

The lineage is also shared rather than parallel.  Haines \emph{et al.}\ build on an
invariant imbedding formulation \cite{bellman1959,mccoy1986}, which is the same
tradition Chapter~5 of \cite{nestor1996} works in, and their impedance treatment
begins with \cite{haines1989} for $SH$ and \cite{hainesdehoop1996} for general
hyperbolic systems.  None of those is cited in the thesis either.

\subsection{The correspondence}

Haines \emph{et al.}\ \cite{haines2004} define an impedance operator $\mathbf{Z}$ by
$\sigma^{n}_{i} = -(\mathbf{Z}\cdot\mathbf{v})_{i}$, relating stretched traction
to particle velocity, and show it satisfies a first-order pseudo-differential
Riccati equation
\begin{equation}
  \partial_{\xi_3}\mathbf{Z} + \mathbf{Z}A_1\mathbf{Z} + \mathbf{Z}A_2
    + A_3\mathbf{Z} + A_4 = 0 ,
  \label{eq:hainesriccati}
\end{equation}
independent of the boundary conditions and solved as an initial value problem,
after which the particle velocity follows from a one-way equation
$\partial_{\xi_3}\mathbf{V} = (A_1 - A_2\mathbf{Z})\mathbf{V}$.

Set against \eqref{eq:impedance}, $\bY' = \bA_{21} + \bA_{22}\bY - \bY\bA_{11}
- \bY\bA_{12}\bY$: the two are the same equation.  The quadratic term is
$\mathbf{Z}A_1\mathbf{Z}$ against $\bY\bA_{12}\bY$, the two linear terms
correspond one for one, and the inhomogeneous term is $A_4$ against $\bA_{21}$.
The marched variable differs only in that $\bY$ maps displacement to traction
where $\mathbf{Z}$ maps particle velocity to traction, a factor $-i\omega$; the
sign conventions differ correspondingly.

\emph{The lateral treatment corresponds too}, and this is the part worth being
plain about.  Haines \emph{et al.} represent horizontal variation in a complete
orthonormal basis, typically Fourier, so that the partial differential equation
becomes a system of ordinary differential equations in $\xi_3$ for the
coefficients and the impedance operator becomes a finite matrix under
truncation.  That is what \S\ref{sec:latop} does with a pseudospectral grid and
\S\ref{sec:threed} with two of them.  Carrying the lateral coupling inside the
marched variable, rather than by an outer iteration on
$(\bI-\bP\,\dCeff)$, is \emph{their} construction, not a departure from the
literature; it is a departure only from the thesis's own architecture
\eqref{eq:cbpdef}, which is what \S\ref{sec:latop} actually compares against.

\emph{The symplectic identity is theirs too, and older than either of us.}
Haines \emph{et al.}\ note in passing, at their eq.~(44), that the system matrix
satisfies $\bA^{T}\bJ = -\bJ\bA$ in both elastic and anelastic media --- which
is \eqref{eq:latsymp} and \eqref{eq:symp3d}, since
$(\bJ\bA)^{T} = \bA^{T}\bJ^{T} = -\bA^{T}\bJ = \bJ\bA$ --- and attribute it to
\cite{kennett1978}, \cite{chapman1994} and \cite{hainesdehoop1996}, remarking that
each such symmetry reflects a conservation law.  It is a 1978
result used here as a discretisation check, not a new identity.  Thomson
\cite{thomson2015b} later develops the same operator for laterally heterogeneous
anisotropic media --- there called a displacement-to-traction operator and
identified as the elastic analogue of a Dirichlet-to-Neumann map --- and proves
that its kernel equals its transpose with the arguments reversed, together with
the symplectic orthogonality of the lateral modes that stands behind the $\bJ_6$
pairing used throughout.

\subsection{What is left}

Setting the formulation aside, three things in this document are not in the
papers above, and they are smaller claims than the one they replace.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The contrast operator.}  $\dA$ and $\dCeff$ are derived from the
thesis's own \texttt{dCBC}/\texttt{dCBCrho} and carry its $\Oh$ structure; the
march is driven by that operator rather than by a general anisotropic medium.
The comparison in \S\ref{sec:latop} is against the thesis architecture
\eqref{eq:cbpdef}, which the literature above does not treat.
\item \textbf{Verification against an exact scatterer.}  The march is scored
against the elastic Mie sphere at fourth order in the depth step
(\S\ref{sec:threed}), on a sharp boundary, through the Poisson array relation.
The papers above establish the formulation and its accuracy in their own terms;
this is an independent external check of the same construction.
\item \textbf{Why the impedance is better \emph{conditioned}.}  Haines
\emph{et al.}\ do argue for the impedance over an up--down split, but on
different grounds: the decomposition into upgoing and downgoing parts ``can
become poorly defined for laterally varying media'', whereas decomposing by the
direction of energy \emph{flow} gives something that ``exists and is
well-defined for all wavefields''.  That is a statement about well-definedness,
and the difficulty they name is the lateral coupling --- a two-dimensional
medium scatters one wavenumber into all of them, and $P$ at a given wavenumber
may be evanescent while $S$ at the same wavenumber still propagates.  They do
not discuss eigenvector degeneracy, conditioning or loss of precision.  The
argument of \S\ref{sec:impedance} is therefore a different one and is measured:
$\bV$ needs the \emph{full} inverse $\mathbf{D}_z^{-1}$, which mixes the two
families and is where the cancellation lives, while $\bY$ needs only the three
downgoing columns, so its residual tracks
$\mathrm{cond}(\mathbf{U}_d)\,\epsilon$ linearly to $7.2\times10^{-10}$ over
$|\mathbf{k}|=2$ to $6000$ where $\bV$ returns $2.000$ --- a lost branch, not a
lost digit.  The two arguments are complementary: theirs is that the
decomposition may fail to exist, this one is what computing it anyway costs.
\item \textbf{The array coupling as a measurement.}  The residual after the
depth error is exhausted is inter-sphere multiple scattering, linear in the
contrast at $1.45\,\varepsilon$ over two decades --- a number about the
periodic problem the march actually solves, not about the march.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The integrator is theirs as well, and better}

The fourth-order result of \S\ref{sec:whatimbedbuys} is \emph{not} a contrast
with this literature.  Haines \emph{et al.}\ solve both the Riccati and the
one-way equation with ``multiply linked applications of the explicit
fourth-order Runge--Kutta scheme'', and state the stage requirement outright:
``for a step size of $h$, the Runge--Kutta scheme requires function values at
$\xi_3$, $\xi_3+\tfrac12 h$ and $\xi_3+h$: in other words at half-step
intervals''.  That is the scheme of Algorithm~1 and the sampling discipline of
\S\ref{sec:whatimbedbuys}.  The $32\times$ of \S\ref{sec:whatimbedbuys} is
measured against a \emph{layer stack}, which is a different comparison and not
one with them.

On three further counts their implementation was ahead of the one described
here.  All three have since been adopted, and \S\ref{sec:tricks} reports what
each was worth when measured rather than estimated --- which in one case is a
good deal less than the estimate below, left standing so the correction is
visible:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Adaptive step control.}  They derive a step size from the leading
Runge--Kutta error, $h = \theta/(\|\mathbf{D}\| +
\sqrt{\theta\,\|\partial_{\xi_3}\mathbf{D}\|})$ with
$\mathbf{D}=-i\omega(\bA^{VV}-\bA^{VT}\mathbf{Z})$, reporting relative
precisions of about $1$ part in $4\times10^{3}$ and $10^{5}$ at
$\theta=\tfrac12$ and $\tfrac14$.  The march here uses a uniform step and meets
the explicit method's stability wall at $2|k|_{\max}h \simeq 1.3$ with no
adaptive rule at all.
\item \textbf{Assembling $\bA$ by FFT.}  ``The elements can be computed by fast
Fourier transform when using Fourier series as the basis functions.''  Here
$\mathbf{D}_x$ and $\mathbf{D}_y$ are built as dense Kronecker matrices.  The
saving is bounded --- the quadratic term $\bY\bA_{12}\bY$ is dense in any basis
and is $69\%$ of the work by \S\ref{sec:latcost}, so the ceiling is about
$1.45\times$ --- but it is a saving not taken.
\item \textbf{Reuse.}  ``Once the impedance operators and fundamental solutions
have been calculated for a given region of heterogeneity they can be used in
solving any number of problems (allowing, for example, varying source and
receiver geometries) involving that region.''  Nothing here exploits that.
\end{itemize}

They also make explicit a stability argument used only implicitly here: the
one-way equation is integrated \emph{in} the direction of energy flow and the
Riccati equation \emph{against} it, and that choice is what keeps both stable.

The M\"obius step and its replacement in \S\ref{sec:algorithm} are therefore not
an improvement on this literature either.  Haines \emph{et al.}\ form no matrix
exponential anywhere; the exponential is inherited from the layer-stack
tradition, and removing it recovers a position they already held.

\subsection{The three counts, adopted and measured}\label{sec:tricks}

Each of the three was built into the march and scored against the version
without it.  The gate is \verb|gate_first_order_lateral_impedance_3d.py|, now
$56/56$.

\paragraph{Adaptive step control, and what it really controls.}  Their rule is
implemented as written, with $\mathbf{D}=\bA_{11}+\bA_{12}\bY$ --- the operator
of the one-way equation $\bu'=(\bA_{11}+\bA_{12}\bY)\bu$, which is their
$-i\omega(\bA^{VV}-\bA^{VT}\mathbf{Z})$ in the conventions of this note --- and
$\partial_z\mathbf{D}$ by one finite difference at the start of each step.
Tightening $\theta$ tightens the answer as it should:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
\toprule
$\theta$ & steps & relative error & mean $2|k|_{\max}h$\\
\midrule
$1$            & $21$ & $4.18\times10^{-5}$ & $0.24$\\
$\tfrac12$     & $39$ & $3.06\times10^{-6}$ & $0.13$\\
$\tfrac14$     & $75$ & $2.18\times10^{-7}$ & $0.07$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\captionof{table}{Adaptive stepping on the 3-D lateral grid, scored against a
converged uniform march.  Halving $\theta$ buys about $14\times$, consistent
with the fourth-order error the rule is derived from.}
\end{center}

\noindent
Against a uniform step at equal accuracy the saving is $75$ steps rather than
$128$ --- worth having, and smaller than the headline.  The more useful
observation is structural, and it is not one this note made before implementing
the rule: \textbf{the step control is also the stability control.}  Since
$h \le \theta/\|\mathbf{D}\|$ and $\|\mathbf{D}\|$ bounds the largest vertical
wavenumber, $2|k|_{\max}h \le 2\theta$; at $\theta=\tfrac12$ that is $1.0$,
inside the explicit method's wall at $\simeq1.3$ measured in
\S\ref{sec:whatimbedbuys}.  This is why \cite{haines2004} can use an explicit
Runge--Kutta scheme throughout and meet no stability wall, where the uniform
march here did.  The wall was never an argument for an implicit step; it was an
argument for a step-size rule.

One honest qualification.  $\|\mathbf{D}\|$ is taken as the maximum absolute row
sum, which bounds the spectral radius at $O(n^2)$ rather than the cost of a
decomposition --- and measured on the operator at the base of the slab it
over-bounds it by $4.4\times$.  That factor is paid in steps.  A tighter
estimate would recover it; an eigendecomposition would cost more than it saves.

\paragraph{Fourier assembly of $\bA$, which is worth much less than estimated.}
Every factor of $\mathbf{D}_x$ or $\mathbf{D}_y$ is applied to the columns of
the identity by transform instead of multiplied in, so a term such as
$\mathbf{D}_x\zeta\mathbf{D}_x$ costs $O(N^{2}\log N)$ rather than $O(N^{3})$.
It reproduces the dense assembly to $4.4\times10^{-16}$.  It is also
\emph{slower} than the dense product below $N\approx100$, where the transform's
constant dominates, and reaches $1.8$--$2.5\times$ at $N=144$.

The estimate above --- a ceiling of about $1.45\times$ --- was too generous, and
for a reason worth stating.  It compared assembly against assembly.  Assembly is
not the step: one Runge--Kutta step is four evaluations of the right-hand side
at $13.7$\,ms each against $6.2$\,ms of assembly, so assembly is $10\%$ of the
step and a $2\times$ saving on it is $5\%$ of the march.  The reason is the one
\S\ref{sec:dtn} gives structurally: $\bY\bA_{12}\bY$ is a Schur complement and
is dense in every basis, so no transform reaches the term that dominates.  The
trick is real, it is now taken, and it is worth a few per cent rather than half.

\paragraph{Reuse, which this formulation gets for nothing.}  $\bY$ is the
Dirichlet-to-Neumann map of the medium and contains no reference to a source, so
one march serves any number of them: $425$\,ms for the march, then eight sources
in $2.0$\,ms in total, against $3400$\,ms to treat each as a fresh problem.
Nothing had to be added for this beyond noticing it, and the same property in a
second guise --- $\bY$ as a complete state, so that regions compose by handing
over one matrix --- is what \S\ref{sec:dtn} gates as the imbedding restart.

\medskip
\noindent
The one-way equation those fundamental solutions would be assembled from is
now built --- \S\ref{sec:rt} --- so what remains theirs and not taken is the
assembly and reuse of the solutions themselves, and their treatment of varying
receiver geometry.

\subsection{What has not been checked}

The correspondence above is drawn from the full text of \cite{haines2004}, and
from the abstract and stated results of \cite{thomson2015b} only.  The companion
paper \cite{hulme2004}, which carries the worked examples and the numerical
validation, has not been consulted, so no comparison of accuracy or run time
against their implementation is offered here --- and on the evidence of their
\S2.5 there is no reason to expect one to favour the march described above.

One genuine difference of construction remains unresolved.  Haines \emph{et al.}
handle material discontinuities with a curvilinear transformation, $\xi_3$
constant on major interfaces, so that tractions and velocities stay continuous
and are never differentiated across a jump.  \S\ref{sec:threed} instead takes
the cross-section through its exact transform \eqref{eq:discff} and marches the
sphere's own bounding slab, which earns fourth order across a sharp boundary
with no transform at all --- but only because the disc's \emph{area} is
polynomial in depth.  That is a device for this problem, not a method for
interfaces, and whether the two coincide in any useful sense has not been
established.

\section{What the contrast parameter scales}\label{sec:epsscaling}

Every sphere measurement in this note is indexed by a single number
$\varepsilon$, and several of the conclusions are statements about how something
depends on it --- the array coupling is linear in $\varepsilon$
(\S\ref{sec:threed}), the Krylov iteration count grows with it
(\S\ref{sec:vsfoldy}).  Those are only readable if it is clear what
$\varepsilon$ moves.  It is defined in \S\ref{sec:threed} as the fractional
perturbation of $\alpha$, $\beta$ and $\rho$ inside the sphere; this appendix
records what that forces everything else to do, because the answer is not
$\varepsilon$ and in one place is nearly five times it.

\paragraph{The family.}  Put $s = 1+\varepsilon$ and scale all three primary
quantities by it, $\alpha'=s\alpha$, $\beta'=s\beta$, $\rho'=s\rho$.  The moduli
are then fixed, not free:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sfamily}
  \rho' = s\rho, \qquad
  \mu' = \rho'\beta'^{2} = s^{3}\mu, \qquad
  \lambda' = \rho'\alpha'^{2}-2\mu' = s^{3}\lambda .
\end{equation}
This is the construction the sphere gate already carries, and it is derived
there rather than typed, because the march and the Mie arbiter must describe the
same sphere and a hand conversion between $(\alpha,\beta,\rho)$ and
$(\lambda,\mu,\rho)$ is a silent factor waiting to happen.

\paragraph{What follows, and why one exponent is easy to miss.}  Everything else
in the problem is a power of $s$:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccccc@{}}
\toprule
$\varepsilon$ & $0.02$ & $0.10$ & $0.20$ & $0.40$ & $0.80$\\
\midrule
$\Delta\alpha/\alpha=\Delta\beta/\beta=\Delta\rho/\rho \;=\; s-1$
  & $0.020$ & $0.100$ & $0.200$ & $0.400$ & $0.800$\\
$\Delta\lambda/\lambda=\Delta\mu/\mu \;=\; s^{3}-1$
  & $0.061$ & $0.331$ & $0.728$ & $1.744$ & $\mathbf{4.832}$\\
$\Delta Z/Z \;=\; s^{2}-1$, \quad $Z=\rho c$
  & $0.040$ & $0.210$ & $0.440$ & $0.960$ & $2.240$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\captionof{table}{The moduli move as the \emph{cube}.  At $\varepsilon=0.8$ ---
the strongest point of the Krylov sweep in \S\ref{sec:vsfoldy} --- the shear
modulus inside the sphere is nearly six times the background, not $1.8$ times,
and that is what the iteration count is responding to.}
\end{center}

\noindent
Two consequences are worth naming.  \textbf{Poisson's ratio is unchanged}:
$\alpha/\beta$ is $s$-independent by construction, so the family moves the
\emph{scale} of the medium and not its elastic character, and a sweep in
$\varepsilon$ is a clean one-parameter sweep rather than a path through a
two-parameter space.  And \textbf{the impedance moves as $s^{2}$}, which is the
quantity reflection actually responds to --- so the single-interface coefficient
$(Z'-Z)/(Z'+Z)$ is $(s^{2}-1)/(s^{2}+1)$, exactly $\varepsilon$ to leading order
and only that to leading order.

\paragraph{A symbol warning.}  $\varepsilon$ is overloaded in this note: bare and
without indices it is the contrast of \eqref{eq:sfamily}; bold
($\boldsymbol\varepsilon$, $\boldsymbol\varepsilon_{i3}$) or carrying indices
($\varepsilon_{33}$) it is strain.  The usage is consistent throughout and the
two never appear in the same expression, but they are not the same object and
the distinction is carried entirely by the typography.
