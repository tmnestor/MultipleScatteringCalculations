\title{\bfseries Polynomial Voxels on an Octree for Elastic Waves\\[4pt]
\large A Grid Grown and Judged by an Error Known before the Solve}
\author{T. M. Nestor\\[2pt]\small Independent researcher, Australia}
\date{Draft, 23 November 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
A volume integral equation for elastic waves is usually solved on a uniform grid of voxels, fine
everywhere because it must be fine somewhere. This paper puts the voxels on an octree, each leaf a cube
that carries polynomials for the wavefield and for the medium, and chooses the tree by the error it will
have. The companion paper \citep{NestorError2026} showed that the far-field error of such a scheme is
two terms: the error of its first-order (Born) response, which needs no solve, and the nonlinear part of
the response times the relative projection error of the medium on the cells. Here both terms are
computed for a tree with nothing solved, the second from one product with the tree's matrix, and a tree
is grown by them from a grid of two cells across. On three radial bodies with exact solutions (a sphere
with a graded shell, a strong centre in a weak surround, and the classical uniform sphere with a sharp
surface) the two terms give the solved error of the grown trees to $1.3\%$ on the smooth bodies and to
$5\%$ on the sharp one. The product with the matrix of a tree is made exact and matrix-free: a leaf is a
union of cells of the smallest size, on which its polynomials are polynomials of the same degree, so the
product is a spread, one convolution by fast Fourier transform, and a gather. With it trees of the sharp
sphere are solved to $16\,000$ leaves, where a tree needs between a quarter and a third of the cells of
a uniform grid of the same error; on the two smooth bodies the largest trees need from a third of the
cells to all of them. The saving is real and modest, and the paper says why: a constant cell
must resolve the wave wherever there is contrast, and at a surface that cuts the cells the scheme is of
first order for cells of any degree, with the degree and the refinement interchangeable. What the method
offers is less a small grid than a known one: the error of every candidate grid is available before
anything is solved.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

\paragraph{The problem.} A heterogeneous elastic body in a uniform background scatters an incident wave,
and the scattered field satisfies a volume integral equation over the body with the Green's tensor of
the background as its kernel. Divided into cubic voxels, the equation becomes a dense linear system
whose matrix couples every voxel to every other. On a uniform grid the coupling depends on the
separation of two voxels alone, the product with the matrix is a convolution, and the fast Fourier
transform makes systems of millions of unknowns routine. The price is the uniform grid itself: its cell
is set by the most demanding place in the body and is paid for everywhere.

\paragraph{What is known.} Polynomial cells, and adaptive trees of them, are not new. For the scalar
Lippmann--Schwinger equation in two dimensions, \citet{Ambikasaran2015} discretise on an adaptive quad
tree with a polynomial on every leaf, splitting a leaf until it holds a set number of points per
wavelength and resolves the contrast to a tolerance, and \citet{Gopal2020} accelerate the direct solve.
In three dimensions \citet{Malhotra2015,Malhotra2016} represent volume sources by polynomials of bounded
total degree on the leaves of an adaptive octree, refined where the highest coefficients exceed a
tolerance, and evaluate their potentials with a kernel-independent fast multipole method
\citep{Ying2004} for the Poisson, Stokes and low-frequency Helmholtz problems, at up to $10^{10}$
unknowns. In electromagnetics, large volume elements carry fields of high polynomial order
\citep{Chobanyan2013}, with the material represented to an order of its own \citep{Chobanyan2015}, and
voxels with a degree-one field are solved with fast Fourier transforms \citep{Georgakis2020}. Where
error is estimated for such methods it is estimated \emph{a posteriori}, from a computed solution that
is then refined and solved again \citep{Beilina2022}. For elastic waves the volume integral equation is
solved on uniform grids at low order \citep{Shekhar2023}.

\paragraph{What this paper adds.} In each of those methods the tree is refined by a property of the
medium or of a computed solution, and the error of the result is found by solving. The companion paper
\citep{NestorError2026}, cited below as Paper~I with its equations, tables and sections prefixed by
\emph{I}, gives the error of a grid of polynomial voxels in a form known before the solve, and this
paper turns that law into a method:
\begin{enumerate}
\item the error of a tree is computed from two terms with nothing solved, and the computation is tested
against the solved error on three bodies (\S\ref{sec:grown});
\item a tree is grown from two cells across by the error it will have, without being told whether the
wave or the medium sets that error (\S\ref{sec:where}, \S\ref{sec:grown});
\item the case of a surface that cuts the cells is worked through: the scheme is of first order there,
for constant and for degree-one cells, and the law gives the size of the error (\S\ref{sec:jump});
\item the product with the matrix of a tree is made exact and matrix-free, which carries the trees beyond
the reach of a dense solver (\S\ref{sec:product});
\item what a tree saves over a uniform grid is measured on solved errors, for constant and for
degree-one leaves (\S\ref{sec:saves}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Relation to the adaptive integral method.} The \emph{adaptive integral method} of
\citet{Bleszynski1996} and the precorrected fast Fourier transform \citep{Phillips1997} answer a
different question from this paper's. They are fast products for a fixed discretisation: the sources are projected onto a regular grid, in the first by matching their multipole
moments and in the second by matching the potentials they produce at a set of test points, the
interactions between distant cells are evaluated on that grid by fast Fourier transforms, and those
between near cells are computed directly and corrected. The product of \S\ref{sec:product} is of that
family, and simpler, because the leaves of an octree are unions of cells of a regular grid: nothing is
projected and nothing is corrected, and the product is exact. What is adaptive here is the grid, and
what chooses it is the error.

\paragraph{What is not claimed.} The product of \S\ref{sec:product} costs what a product on the uniform
grid of the smallest cells costs. A tree therefore has fewer unknowns than that grid and not a cheaper
product, and the comparisons of this paper are of cells and unknowns, not of time. All three test bodies
are radial, since that is where exact solutions are to be had. Both limits are taken up in
\S\ref{sec:cautions}.

\section{The scheme and its error law}\label{sec:setting}

The scheme is that of Paper~I, and only what is needed to read the tables is repeated here. The body is
a sphere of radius $a$ in an isotropic background, with a contrast in the Lam\'e moduli and the density
that is a function $f$ of the radius times a fixed contrast; an incident P wave is scattered, and the
far field is compared with the exact solution at nine angles, as the largest difference over the angles
divided by the peak of the exact far field. That is the \emph{error} of every table. The frequency is
given as $k_Sa$, the shear wavenumber of the background times the radius.

\paragraph{Cells.} The body is covered by cubes, the leaves of an octree; leaf $\ell$ has centre
$\bx_\ell$ and half-width $h_\ell$. On each leaf the nine-component state (displacement and strain) is
expanded in the Legendre products of total degree at most $p$, and the contrast in those of degree at
most $r$; this paper takes $p=r$, with $p=0$ (constant cells, $9$ unknowns a leaf) or $p=1$ (degree-one
cells, $36$). The system is the Galerkin one of \S I.\ref{E-sec:cells3d}, its blocks integrated in closed
form, and a block between leaves of different sizes is an exact finite sum of blocks between equal cells.

\paragraph{The law.} Write the scheme's response as its Born series in the contrast. The error of the
far field is then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:law}
\text{error}\;=\;\delta T_1\;+\;\text{rest},\qquad \text{rest}\;\approx\;\nu\,F\,\cE ,
\end{equation}
in which $\delta T_1$ is the error of the first-order term, the scheme's Born far field less the exact
one; $\nu$ is the nonlinear fraction of the response, the part of the exact far field beyond first
order as a fraction of its peak; $\cE$ is the relative projection error of the medium, the energy of
what the cells' polynomials of degree $r$ cannot hold of $f$ divided by the energy of $f$
(Eq.~(I.\ref{E-eq:proj})); and $F$ is a factor of order one that Paper~I derives. The first term is of
order $2p+2$ in the cell and depends on the wave; the second is of order $2r+2$ on a smooth medium and
depends on the medium. The first is a sum over the leaves of known amplitudes, each leaf holding the
projected incident wave, so it needs no solve (\S I.\ref{E-sec:born}).

\section{Where to refine}\label{sec:where}

If the error of the nonlinear part were a
fixed multiple of the projection error of the medium, a grid would be refined where the medium's detail
is large and left coarse where the medium is uniform. Equation~(I.\ref{E-eq:slope}) says that this is safe
only while $kL\ll1$: in a uniform region the medium has no detail and the product of medium and wave
does. One experiment tests the point (\path{scripts/measure_octree_product_indicator.py}). The sphere of

exceeds a tolerance. Two indicators are compared: the energy of the leaf's detail coefficients of the

\paragraph{How to read it.} At $k_Sa=4$ take the three trees refined by the medium. From $856$ to $1696$ leaves
every added cell is in the shell, where the medium varies, and the error does not fall: $0.071$, $0.059$,
$0.072$. The sixteen leaves left in the core are each two radians of shear phase across, and they set the
error whatever is done to the shell. The tree refined by the product has divided half of them, and with
$912$ leaves its error is $0.043$: less than the medium's rule reaches with twice as many.

\paragraph{What it shows, and what it does not.} Three things. At $k_Sa=0.5$ the two rules build the same
trees down to $856$ leaves, as they must when $kL\ll1$, and part company only at the tightest
tolerances, where the product's rule divides half the core leaves and the error is a quarter lower
($4.42\times10^{-4}$ against $5.75\times10^{-4}$). At $k_Sa=4$ the medium's detail alone is the wrong
guide and the product's is a better one. At $k_Sa=2$ the product's rule gains nothing: its trees are no
better at an equal number of leaves. The reason is in the last column. At $k_Sa=2$ and $4$ the error of
constant cells on this body \emph{is} the first-order error, to $2.5\%$ on every tree at $k_Sa=2$ and to
$5\%$ at $k_Sa=4$, under either rule; and the first-order error is a sum of amplitudes with phases, not
of energies, so no indicator built from an energy ranks the trees reliably. What does rank them there is
the first-order error itself, which needs no solve. For constant cells on a body not small against the
wavelength, then, a grid of mixed sizes can be chosen before anything is solved by evaluating that term
on the candidates; the detail of the product says where to look, and Eq.~(I.\ref{E-eq:slope}) says why the
detail of the medium does not. At $k_Sa=0.5$ the two terms are comparable and partly cancel, and the
first-order error alone does not rank the two finest trees.

\section{A tree grown by its error, with no solve}\label{sec:grown}

\paragraph{A tree grown by the first-order error.} The last observation is a method, and it was tried
(\path{scripts/measure_octree_first_order_greedy.py}). The first-order error of a tree is a known vector
over a set of directions, the sum of the leaves' Born far fields less the exact one, and refining a leaf
changes it by a known amount. Starting from two cells across, the one refinement that most reduces that
vector is made, again and again, with nothing solved; growing a tree of $1700$ leaves takes some tens of
seconds. Because a tree has hundreds of refinements to choose among, it could cancel the error at a few
directions without being a better grid, so it is grown on $40$ directions spread over the whole sphere
and judged on the nine of Table~I.\ref{E-tab:twoterm}, which the growth never sees.

\paragraph{Both terms with no solve.} The term that is missing can also be had without solving. The scheme's
response of second order in the contrast is the second term of its own Born series, which is one product
of the assembled matrix with the first-order state (the projected incident wave in every leaf); its
far field less the exact term of second order (a second difference of the exact solution in the contrast)
is the second-order error $\delta T_2$ of the tree. The matrix must be assembled, which is the cost of one
iteration of an iterative solver, but nothing is solved. The prediction is then $|\delta T_1+\delta T_2|$,
a sum of complex vectors over the directions and not of magnitudes, since at $k_Sa=0.5$ the two partly
cancel. Table~\ref{tab:greedytwo} sets it beside the solved error on three bodies. The first is the sphere
of Table~I.\ref{E-tab:twoterm}. The second, called the halo, has the full contrast inside $0.2a$, falls over
$0.2a<r<0.4a$ to a tenth of it, keeps a tenth out to $0.8a$ and falls to zero at the surface. The third is
the classical uniform sphere with a sharp surface, on which a constant cell cut by the surface holds the
contrast times the fraction of its volume inside (computed exactly along one axis and by a midpoint rule
of $64\times64$ points across the other two, since a Gauss rule cannot integrate a step). All three are
radial, so the exact solution is known.

\paragraph{A rule that does not stall, and what a tree saves.} The uniform sphere at $k_Sa=4$ broke the rule
of single best steps. The grown tree kept $24$ large leaves in the interior, each two radians of shear
phase across, and its first-order error only crept from $0.062$ to $0.050$ between $600$ leaves and $4000$, while a uniform
grid of $2728$ cells has $0.019$. The errors of those leaves largely cancel in the sum, so dividing any
one of them alone makes the total larger, though dividing all of them makes it much smaller; a rule that
takes the single best step never takes that one. Dividing instead the leaf whose own first-order error is
largest cannot stall in this way, but it gives up the cancellation and its error does not fall steadily.
The two are joined by one test: a leaf whose own error exceeds a set fraction of the error of the whole
tree can only be so large because other leaves are cancelling it, and that leaf is divided; otherwise the
single best step is taken. The fraction was set to a quarter on the case that stalled (a half and one do
not release it) and then run unchanged on the other seven cases. From $1300$ leaves up it is within $5\%$ of the
better of the two simpler rules in every case; at $600$ leaves it is a tenth and a fifth worse in two
cases. Table~\ref{tab:greedybodies} gives what it saves.

cells to the power $-0.7$ (second order in the cell size), so a ratio of $0.6$ is the same error from half
the cells and a ratio of $0.5$ the same error from three eighths of them. That is the size of the saving
on all three bodies: the same error from two fifths to three fifths of the cells, not from a tenth of
them. The reason is in
Eq.~(I.\ref{E-eq:slope}). A constant cell loses the product of medium and wave, and in the interior of a
uniform body, where the medium loses nothing, the wave part $(kh)^2$ remains; a tree can coarsen there
only as far as the wavelength allows. The sharp surface does not change this as much as one might expect,
because a cut cell that holds its exact volume fraction has a first-order error of second order in its
size, like every other cell. Only where the wave part rules everywhere, as on the first body at $k_Sa=4$,
does a tree save nothing.

\section{A surface that cuts the cells}\label{sec:jump}

first-order errors, and on the sharp sphere at low frequency the first-order error is not the error. At

What is different about the jump is the rate. On a smooth body $\cE$ falls as the square of the cell size
for constant cells; on the sharp sphere it is the volume of the cut cells, weighted by $\phi(1-\phi)$
with $\phi$ the fraction of a cell inside, and it falls as the first power ($0.238$, $0.165$, $0.123$,
$0.083$ are $0.95$, $0.99$, $0.98$ and $0.99$ divided by the number of cells across). The first term is of
second order on both. So on a body with a sharp boundary the second term must overtake the first as the
grid is refined, at any frequency, and Table~\ref{tab:greedytwo} shows it doing so first where the body
is small against the wavelength. This is not the familiar loss of order at a staircase boundary, which
the volume fractions remove from the first-order term; it is a statement about the nonlinear part, and
the law gives its size before anything is solved.

A rule that grows the tree by both terms was tried on the sharp sphere, minimising
$|\delta T_1|^2+(c\,\cE)^2$ with $c$ measured on the tree just grown from its $\delta T_2$. It builds
nearly the same trees as the first-order rule and gains nothing ($9.41\times10^{-4}$ on $2201$ leaves at
$k_Sa=0.5$ against $9.29\times10^{-4}$ on $2203$), and the table says why: the first-order rule had
already refined the surface to a half-width of $a/16$ by $1300$ leaves, where the tree's $\cE=0.064$ is
about that of the uniform grid of $16$ across ($0.062$ by the first-power rule), which has $2728$ cells. The tree does save on the second term, then, by the
same half of the cells as on the first. To do better the cut cells must go to a half-width of $a/32$, of which there are
several thousand, more than the dense solver can take. This is the case in which a tree should save most,
since the cut cells grow in number as the inverse square of their size and the interior cells as the
inverse cube, and it needs a solver whose cost does not grow as the cube of the number of leaves. That
solver, and the trees it reaches, are below (Table~\ref{tab:treesfree}).

\paragraph{Degree-one cells on the sharp sphere.} A cell of degree one cannot follow a jump either. Its
medium is the projection of the indicator of the sphere onto the polynomials of degree one on the cell,
which the experiment takes from the exact means of the indicator and of the indicator times each local
coordinate $\boldsymbol\xi$: with $\phi$ the fraction of the cell inside and
$\mathbf m=\langle\chi\,\boldsymbol\xi\rangle$, the cell holds $\phi+3\,\mathbf m\cdot\boldsymbol\xi$,
and what it loses is $\phi-\phi^2-3\lvert\mathbf m\rvert^2$ times its volume, where a constant cell loses
$\phi-\phi^2$. That is smaller, by a factor of about $0.45$ on these grids, and it falls at the same

\section{An exact matrix-free product for a tree}\label{sec:product}

On an octree the cells differ in size and
the blocks no longer depend on an offset alone, so the solver for trees assembles its matrix densely and
stops near $20\,000$ unknowns. The convolution is recovered one level down. Let $h$ be the smallest
half-width in the tree. A leaf of half-width $2^jh$ is the union of $8^j$ cells of half-width $h$, and
on each such cell $d$ the leaf's field functions $Q_a$ and source monomials $m_c$ are polynomials of
the same degree in the cell's own coordinate,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reexpand}
Q_a^{\text{leaf}}=\sum_b C^{(d)}_{ab}\,Q_b^{(d)},\qquad m_c^{\text{leaf}}=\sum_f D^{(d)}_{cf}\,m_f^{(d)},
\end{equation}
with $C^{(d)}$ and $D^{(d)}$ depending only on $j$ and on the place of $d$ within the leaf. The block
between leaves $m$ and $n$ is then
$K_{ac}(m,n)=\sum_{d\in m}\sum_{e\in n}\sum_{bf}C^{(d)}_{ab}\,K_{bf}(d-e)\,D^{(e)}_{cf}$, exactly, with
$K(d-e)$ the block between equal cells of half-width $h$. So the product with the tree's matrix is three
steps: each leaf's source coefficients are spread to its smallest cells by $D^{\mathsf T}$; the sources
on the uniform lattice of smallest cells are convolved with $K$ by FFT, with the kernel and the compiled
product of the uniform solver; and the result is gathered to the leaves by $C$. The mass term and the
right-hand side are each leaf's own, and the preconditioner is block-Jacobi with each leaf's self block
at its own size. The unknowns are those of the tree, while the time of a product and the memory of the
kernel are those of the uniform grid of smallest cells over the cube that bounds the tree; the gain over
the dense solver is in the number of leaves that can be solved, not in the cost of a product. It is
tested against the dense matrix on a tree of three sizes for constant and degree-one cells (agreement to
$4\times10^{-14}$ of the coupling, although the dense solver takes the block between two large leaves at
their own size and this product sums it over their descendants), and against the dense solve and the
dense Born series.

first-order ratio is not a guide in either direction. On the graded shell at $k_Sa=0.5$ a tree with

that the first-order rule neither knows nor controls. Thirdly, nowhere within reach of the dense solver
does a tree of constant cells save an order of magnitude; the most is a factor of about three in cells. The
column of cells is the roughest of the three, since it inverts an interpolation, and on the halo, whose
uniform-grid errors do not fall evenly, it should be read as no better than a guide.

\paragraph{Trees beyond the dense solver.} The convolution that a uniform grid allows is lost when the
cells differ in size, but only at the level of the leaves. A leaf of half-width $2^jh$ is $8^j$ cells
of the smallest half-width $h$ of the tree, and on each of them the leaf's polynomials are polynomials
of the same degree, so the block between any two leaves is a finite sum of blocks between equal cells of
the smallest size, which depend on their offset alone. The product of the tree's matrix with a vector is
therefore a spread of each leaf's source to its smallest cells, one convolution by FFT on the uniform
lattice of those cells, and a gather back to the leaves (\S\ref{sec:product};
\path{cubic_scattering/graded_voxel/octree_fft.py}). Nothing is approximated. On a tree of three sizes
the product agrees with the dense matrix to $4\times10^{-14}$ of the coupling, for constant and
degree-one cells, and on the trees of $603$ and $1301$ leaves of the sharp sphere the first-order error, the
second-order error and the solved error are those of the dense solver to the four figures printed. The
unknowns are the tree's; the time of a product and the memory of the kernel are those of the uniform
grid of the smallest cells, so what is gained is reach and not a cheaper product. A solve takes $7$ to
$9$ products on these trees, as on uniform grids.

\section{Cautions}\label{sec:cautions}

Three bodies, all radial. The trees refined by an indicator (Table~\ref{tab:trees}) rest on the dense
solver, which stops near $2200$ constant leaves and stopped the product's rule at $912$ leaves at
$k_Sa=4$ (its next tree has $2368$); the grown trees and the uniform grids do not. The shares of
Tables~\ref{tab:treesfree}, \ref{tab:treesdeg1} and \ref{tab:treessmooth} invert an interpolation between
uniform grids, and on the halo, whose uniform-grid errors do not fall evenly, they are no better than a
guide. The degree-one trees were held to a smallest half-width of $a/32$ and to $4000$
leaves, and their comparison with constant trees is at equal unknowns, which is not equal time: a
product costs what the uniform grid of the smallest cells costs, at either degree. Against
uniform grids the product's tree on the graded shell at $k_Sa=4$ is a little worse (a uniform grid of
$912$ cells gives $0.039$ by interpolation, the tree $0.043$): at that frequency the wave must be
resolved wherever there is contrast, and on that body a tree has little to save.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}

\paragraph{The error of a tree is known before it is solved.} Both terms of the error law of Paper~I
can be computed for an octree of polynomial voxels with nothing solved: the first from one pass over
the leaves, the second from one product with the tree's matrix. On the trees solved here the two give
the solved error to $1.3\%$ on the two smooth bodies and to $5\%$ on the sharp sphere, and on the
largest trees of the sharp sphere, of $16\,000$ constant leaves, to $4\%$.

\paragraph{A tree can be grown by that error.} Starting from two cells across and dividing the leaf
that most reduces the first-order error, with one test that keeps the rule from stalling, gives trees
within a few per cent of the better of two indicator rules at each frequency without being told which
of the wave and the medium is in charge. For constant cells, adding the second term to the rule gains
nothing; for degree-one cells, whose first-order error is negligible beside the error, it gains up to
a fifth.

\paragraph{A surface that cuts the cells makes the scheme first order.} With each cut cell holding its
exact projection of the medium, the first-order error keeps its order, and the second term, which
follows the projection error, falls only as the first power of the cell. It overtakes the first term
on every grid fine enough, for constant and for degree-one cells, and the law gives its size.

\paragraph{The product with a tree's matrix is exact and matrix-free.} A leaf is a union of cells of
the smallest size, so the product is a spread, one convolution and a gather. It reproduces the dense
matrix to $4\times10^{-14}$ of the coupling. It costs what the uniform grid of the smallest cells
costs, so it extends the reach of the trees and not the speed of a product.

\paragraph{What a tree saves is modest, and the reason is known.} At $2200$ constant leaves a tree
reaches the error of a uniform grid with a third to two thirds of its cells, and on the sharp sphere at
$16\,000$ leaves with a quarter to a third. On the halo the largest trees need a third to three fifths,
and on the graded shell, whose contrast is strong almost everywhere, a tree saves nothing in five cases
of six. A constant cell must resolve the wave wherever there is
contrast, so the interior cannot be made coarse; at a jump the degree and the refinement are
interchangeable, so a higher degree does not rescue the surface. Degree-one leaves gain where the wave
has to be resolved, by up to $3.5$ at equal unknowns on the sharp sphere, through the first-order error
alone. On the two smooth bodies they gain a factor of $6.5$ to $101$ in error at equal unknowns, which is
the gain of the higher order and is had on a uniform grid as well: on a smooth body the degree is the
large factor, and the tree a small one.

\paragraph{What remains.} A body without radial symmetry; leaves of different degrees in one tree, which
the results on the sharp sphere point to; and a product whose cost follows the leaves and not the
smallest cell.
